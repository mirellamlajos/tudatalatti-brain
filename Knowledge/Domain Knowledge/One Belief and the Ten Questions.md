# One Belief and the Ten Questions

> One of the five Copy Foundations docs. Parker reads it before writing or grading any native storytelling ad, advertorial page, VSL, landing page, sales email or long script. It gives the **spine**: what the piece has to make the reader believe, and the order the sections go in. [[Belief Building in Long Copy]] gives the craft inside each section. [[Market Awareness and Sophistication]] sets the opening.

All results quoted from the book are the author's claims [Stated]. His examples come from financial newsletters; the method carries over, the claims and tactics don't (see the override table).

## The idea in one line

A long sales piece works when it plants **one belief** and then answers, in a fixed order, the **ten questions** every reader is quietly asking. Settle the belief first. Then the questions are the outline.

## The one belief

Write this sentence before any copy:

```
[NEW OPPORTUNITY] is the key to [WHAT THE BUYER ALREADY WANTS],
and it's reachable only through [OUR NEW MECHANISM].
```

It does two jobs. It tests whether the idea is strong enough to write at all, and it's the thread every later line gets checked against.

| Slot | What goes in | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **New opportunity** | *What* is different about this way of getting the result. A fresh way of seeing the problem or the route | **The gate.** If the slot can only be filled with advice the market has heard a hundred times, the idea isn't ready. Stop and find a better one |
| **Desire** | Something the buyer already wants. Not something we think they should want | It matches the desire chosen in [[Market Awareness and Sophistication]] |
| **New mechanism** | *How* it's delivered, and why this is the place to get it. It has a proper name | The "only through" is honest: it rests on something that really is the brand's own |

Opportunity is what's different. Mechanism is how it's delivered. Newness alone isn't enough, because nobody knows they need a brand-new thing; it has to be tied to a want they already have.

**Planning routine.** Write the one belief, then answer at least the first seven questions below in a line or two each. That's the map of the whole piece, and it takes minutes. Research and drafting come after the map.

## The ten questions, in order

The reader never says these out loud. The copy answers them anyway, in this order.

| # | The reader is wondering… | The section's job | Technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What's different about this?** | Open on the new opportunity | Make it feel like a discovery being revealed. Show a real object or moment that stands for "new" |
| 2 | **What do I get out of it?** | One big promise tied to the desire, within the first lines | Ask: what promise at the top would make this reader want the next line? |
| 3 | **How do I know it's real?** | Early proof, right after the promise | Tell every proof as a small story: **and, but, therefore** (below). Show proof early without giving away the mechanism |
| 4 | **What's been holding me back?** | Name the real problem that explains why earlier attempts failed, and lift the blame | **Flip the answer to question 1.** If the new thing works because of X, the real problem is that everything else lacks X |
| 5 | **What's to blame?** | Give the real problem a cause outside the reader | Use an enemy the audience already believes in. Never invent one |
| 6 | **Why now?** | Break inertia | A true reason that waiting costs something |
| 7 | **Why should I trust you?** | Credibility | One of three storylines (below) |
| 8 | **How does it work?** | Show how the mechanism produces the promise, so the reader nods along | Hang it on something the reader already believes. Walk it step by step. It's still copy: tell it as a story, don't lecture |
| 9 | **How do I start?** | The offer | A wide, honest gap between what they get and what it costs |
| 10 | **What do I lose if I don't?** | Close without sounding needy | **Three options** (below), then hand the choice back |

**Where things go.** Questions 1 and 2 and the first proof sit at the very top. Questions 1 through 7 make the emotional case and take roughly the first third. Question 8 is the logic that lets the reader justify a choice they've already made on feeling. Urgency (6) is seeded early and returns at the offer.

### And, but, therefore

Every proof, and the mechanism explanation too, is told as a three-beat story:

- **And** sets the scene.
- **But** brings the snag, the doubt, the thing that went wrong.
- **Therefore** lands the result.

The "but" is what makes it a story. A testimonial with no "but" is a claim. A chart with no "but" is a number. The author's backing: a charity study where one child's story raised more money than statistics did, and story plus statistics raised less than the story alone [Stated].

### The flip (question 4)

The real problem isn't invented. It's the mirror image of the opening. Whatever makes the new opportunity new, the real problem is that everything the reader tried before was missing exactly that. Done right, this is where hope starts: *this time could be different, and the last times weren't my fault.*

### Three credibility storylines (question 7)

1. **Been in your shoes.** The guide once had the reader's exact struggle, hit bottom, found the mechanism, and got what the reader wants. The strongest of the three, because it paints the reader's hoped-for life through someone else's story and draws no resistance.
2. **The insider who shares.** Someone found something the big players weren't sharing and hands it to ordinary people.
3. **The expert.** Books, years, events, readers. The fallback when the first two aren't available.

They can be combined.

### The three-options close (question 10)

Restate the belief and the deal, then lay out three roads: **do nothing** and stay where things are, **work it out alone** the slow way, or **take this**. Ask which is easiest. Then give the choice back to the reader. The close presents options and doesn't argue for one; the reader should feel in control.

### Four kinds of resistance

| Resistance | What beats it |
|---|---|
| Feeling pushed | Keep the product out of the lead. Close without need |
| Distrust | Questions 3 and 7 |
| Scrutiny | Question 8 |
| Inertia | Question 6 |

## How much of the ten each format carries

| Format | Carries |
|---|---|
| **VSL, advertorial page, long landing page** | All ten, questions 1–7 in the first third |
| **Native storytelling ad** | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 and 8 inside the story; 6, 9 and 10 compressed into the last lines before the link |
| **Sales email** | 1, 2, one proof (3), then 9 and 10. The page does the rest |
| **Short video script** | 1 and 2, one beat of 3 or 4, then the call to action |
| **Static, headline** | 1 or 2. The one-belief sentence is the brief |

## The ten-question audit (run on every long draft)

Read the draft as the buyer. For each question in order, find the line that answers it.

1. Is there a one-belief sentence on file for this piece, and does every section serve it?
2. Are questions 1 and 2 answered in the first lines?
3. Is there a proof before the reader has to take anything on trust, and does it have a "but"?
4. Is the real problem the flip of the opening?
5. Is the blame on a thing, not a person and never the reader?
6. Does the mechanism explanation come *after* trust is built?
7. Does the close hand the choice back?

A question with no answer is a hole. A question answered out of order is a belief-chain break ([[Belief Building in Long Copy]]).

> **Brain override — the wall rewrites six of the ten steps.** The book's own versions of these steps break the brand's rules. This table is the version the brain uses.
>
> | Step | The book does this | The brain does this |
> |---|---|---|
> | **One belief** | Desire slot invites outcome claims | Desire in claims-safe words: *nyugodtabb*, calmer, more content. No condition, no symptom. The "only through" rests on the brand's own method and materials, never on an outcome |
> | **2 Promise** | Big numeric promises, "you"-first | First or third person. No second-person statement about the reader's health, mind, money or body |
> | **3 Proof** | Aliased testimonials, loose statistics | [F] and [R] sources only outside the cleared native format; every number sourced in the brain |
> | **4 Real problem** | In health offers, tells readers their body is the hidden cause | The cause is an approach or a belief (the wrong advice, the missing first step), told as the narrator's or founder's experience |
> | **5 Blame** | Political groups, drug companies | The motivation industry and its advice. A *what*, not a *who*. Never doctors or medicine |
> | **6 Why now** | Openly builds a false either-or | Real dates, real seat counts, real price changes. If nothing real exists, skip the beat |
> | **7 Trust** | Borrowed authority, hidden-secret framing | The founder's real story and real credentials. Family stories only under the consent rule |
> | **8 How it works** | Makes a claim *feel* logical | Describe the practice and the experience. No account of what it does inside the body outside the cleared native format |
> | **9 Offer** | False close, invented value totals, limited units | Real prices only. No fake anchor, no fake scarcity. No refund talk in native ads |
> | **10 Close** | Raises the pain, shames the ones who don't act | Three options, choice handed back. No shaming line |
>
> The book also holds up con men, cults and pickup artists as models. None of that framing enters the brain. The mechanics underneath are what's kept.
