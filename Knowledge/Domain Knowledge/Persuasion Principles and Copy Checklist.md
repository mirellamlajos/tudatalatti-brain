# Persuasion Principles and Copy Checklist

> One of the five Copy Foundations docs. Parker reads it before writing or grading any copy. The other four decide *what to say and in what order*. This one is the **craft and psychology check**: does the copy tie to a real want, can the reader picture it, is it proven, is it easy to read, is it easy to act on.

Every number in the book is the author's claim, mostly from print readership studies decades old. They're tagged [Stated] and never quoted as facts in anything that ships.

## The five-step frame

1. Pick the want the copy is built on.
2. Build the appeal on it.
3. Write so the reader runs a mental demonstration of having it.
4. Make the claim believable.
5. Ask for action, and make acting easy.

## What people want

**The eight wired desires** are already in `Killer Performance Ads.md` (Life-Force 8): staying alive and enjoying life; food and drink; freedom from fear, pain and danger; companionship; comfortable living; being ahead or keeping up; care of loved ones; social approval.

**Nine learned wants** (weaker than the eight, but real): being informed · curiosity · cleanliness · efficiency · convenience · dependability and quality · beauty and style · economy and profit · bargains.

**The desire formula:** an unmet want creates tension, tension creates desire, desire creates action. The first use of any product happens in the reader's head, so the copy's job is to make them picture using it.

## Principles worth keeping

| Principle | What it is | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Benefit ladder** | People buy for a later payoff, not for the thing | Ask "and what does that give them?" until the answer stops changing. Write to the last rung |
| **Five buyer stages** | Unaware → aware and thinking → wants details → buys → makes it a habit | One piece that covers all five, or a series that walks through them. Check every funnel for a missing stage |
| **Objection first** | Warn the reader about the doubt that's coming, state a fair, brief version of it, then answer it | The reader meets the doubt through your answer. Pull the doubts from real comments and reviews |
| **Belief re-ranking** | It's easier to change how much a belief matters than to change the belief | Never tell readers they're wrong. Show a comparison and let them conclude. The decision should feel like theirs |
| **Two routes** | Costly or personal decisions get thought through; small ones get decided on cues | Big decisions get facts, cases and reasons, in depth. Beliefs formed that way last longer and resist rival pitches |
| **Examples over statistics** | A vivid case triggers feeling and a mental test drive; a list of numbers doesn't | Lead with the example. Use the number to back it, not replace it |
| **Two-sided messaging** | Naming the other side fairly, then saying why yours is better, persuades more than one-sided praise | Include "who this isn't for." Praise what the alternative gets right before showing what it misses |
| **Evidence** | Proof is any fact, object or opinion the advertiser didn't create | If the brand made it up, it isn't proof. This is the same line as the brain's sourcing rule |
| **Proof by volume** | Length, many reviews, many numbered reasons read as strength, even unread | A wall of real reviews; a numbered list of real reasons. Count only what's real |
| **Redundancy, not repetition** | The same message in a new format reads as new; the same ad again irritates | New form and new opening for the same message. Matches the Andromeda rule |
| **Transfer** | Trust borrowed from people and institutions the reader respects | Only with a real endorsement or a real credential |
| **Belonging** | People move toward groups they want to join or already identify with | Show people the reader recognises as their own |
| **Reciprocity** | An unasked gift of real value creates a pull to give back | A free chapter or excerpt given freely, before any ask |
| **Liking and the face** | People buy from people they like; a face looking at the reader draws the eye and builds trust | The founder's face, looking at the camera; the headline as his own words in quote marks |

## Craft rules

**Simplicity.** Short, common words. Short sentences, one thought each. Short paragraphs. One-line paragraphs to pull the eye down. Concrete words over abstract ones. Personal, spoken phrasing.

**Benefits over features.** A feature is a part. A benefit is what the buyer gets. Drill: state the feature, imagine a bored "so what?", answer with what the buyer gains.

**The headline carries the biggest benefit and picks its reader.** Most readers stop at the headline. If two benefits are equal, run the shorter line.

**Safe headline starters:** New · Announcing · Introducing · Presenting · Just released · At last · Finally · Now · Here's · How · This · These.

**Twelve ways into the body copy:**
1. Continue the headline's thought
2. Ask a question
3. Quote an authority
4. Give a free, usable tip
5. Invite the reader to test it
6. Open with the narrator's own early doubt
7. Open with what others say
8. Write it like a report
9. Speak straight to the reader
10. Tell a dramatic story
11. Give very detailed specifics
12. Open with a very short sentence

**Extreme specificity.** Say exactly what, how, with what, in numbers. The one who describes the details wins, even if others do the same thing. (Procedure: [[Testing Doctrine and Specificity]].)

**Concrete, sensory words.** Swap flat wording for words the reader can see.

**Mental movies.** Walk the reader through the experience in as many senses as fit: what it looks like, sounds like, feels like. Scenes sell; facts alone don't.

**Questions.** A question makes the reader want the answer and turns a monologue into a conversation.

**Long copy.** Long, well-written copy outsells short for a considered purchase. A reader who's sold early can stop and buy; the one who needs more gets it. Restate each benefit from several angles.

**Beating inertia.** Two steps: make acting easy, then ask. Say plainly that ordering is easy and list the steps.

**The offer.** A failed ad may mean a weak offer, not a dead product. Test framings (a gift with the order, two for one, a bundle) before blaming the copy. Fix order: headline, then price, then offer.

**Surveys.** Ask buyers what they like, dislike and want, on easy 1–10 scales, plus "what would you change if this were yours?" The answers feed review mining.

**Clever is not effective.** A headline should say what's on offer, to whom and why it matters, with no puzzle to solve.

## Legibility for 50+ readers

- Body text large: 14 pt or more on pages.
- Dark text on a white or light background. Light-on-dark only for a few large words.
- No all-caps headlines beyond a few words.
- Two typefaces at most.
- Headline under the picture. Caption every picture on a page; captions get read.
- One clear path for the eye.

`Advertising to Older Audiences.md` owns this topic; these rules add to it.

## Pre-ship checklist

**Opening**
- [ ] Carries the biggest benefit or the chosen identification move
- [ ] Picks out the right reader
- [ ] Nothing clever that needs solving

**First lines**
- [ ] Uses one of the twelve ways in
- [ ] Flows from the opening
- [ ] Forces the next line

**Body**
- [ ] Built on one named want
- [ ] Benefits, not features; laddered to the last rung
- [ ] At least one scene the reader can see
- [ ] Specific: no vague line where a real number or detail exists
- [ ] Evidence the brand didn't make up
- [ ] The main doubt raised and answered
- [ ] Says who it isn't for, where the format has room
- [ ] Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs
- [ ] One product or one bundle per piece

**Close**
- [ ] Says what to do, in simple steps
- [ ] Any deadline or limit is real
- [ ] Easy to act on

**Layout (pages and statics)**
- [ ] A person looking at the reader, where a person is shown
- [ ] Legibility rules above
- [ ] Picture tied to the message

## What was left out of the book on purpose

About a third of it is print and direct-mail media buying: ad size and page position, coupon borders, envelopes and stamps, island ads, white space bought around an ad, early-2000s email and banner numbers, price endings in dollars and cents, serif-versus-sans findings for paper. None of it carries to Meta in 2026.

> **Brain override — the wall removes these parts of the book entirely.**
> - **Fear appeals.** The book's first principle builds ads on health and safety scares. Not used. The brand's promise is calm, and illness fear is a health claim by the back door.
> - **Second-person attribute openers.** The starters "Do you," "Can you," "Would you," "If you," the chain of yes-questions, and "Warning" are out for any line touching health, mind, money or body. Questions ship in the first or third person.
> - **Invented deadlines and "only a few left."** A deadline or limit is stated only when it's true.
> - **Made-up figures, borrowed seals, self-written Q&A columns, ads dressed as news reports.** Proof is real or it isn't used. Native format is fine; pretending to be a news outlet isn't.
> - **"What it's worth" and "what others paid."** Only prices actually charged.
> - **Guarantees.** Only one the brand really offers, and never in native storytelling ads.
> - **Implying rivals hide something.** Not used.
> - **His health examples** (blood pressure, eyesight, allergy) are never swiped.
