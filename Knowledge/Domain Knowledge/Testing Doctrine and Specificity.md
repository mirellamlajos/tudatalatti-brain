# Testing Doctrine and Specificity

> One of the five Copy Foundations docs. Parker reads it before writing or grading any copy, and before recommending what to test or scale. It's the oldest of the five sources and the plainest: an ad is a salesperson, and the only score that counts is what a buyer costs.

Every number in the book is the author's claim from a century ago [Stated]. None is quoted as fact in anything that ships.

## The core idea

Advertising is selling in print. Its one job is sales at a known cost. Because results can be traced, every question (which opening, which picture, how long, which offer) can be settled by a small test instead of an opinion. Anything done for pride, amusement or decoration is waste.

**The test for any line:** would this help a salesperson sitting across the table from one buyer?

## Principles

### Measure by cost per buyer
- Judge an ad by what a buyer costs, not by clicks, comments, leads or reach. Cheap attention that doesn't buy is worthless.
- Know what a buyer is worth over time and how fast the cost comes back before scaling.
- Trust no untracked ad, yours or anyone else's. A competitor's ad that looks successful proves nothing unless they measure.
- Expect to be wrong. The appeal the team likes best usually isn't the one that wins.

### Test small, then scale
- Let thousands decide what millions will do. A capped test answers almost any question cheaply.
- Keep testing new approaches beside the proven one without disturbing it.
- Settle arguments with a test, not a meeting.

### The opening selects buyers
- The opening's job is to pick out the people who can be interested, not to draw the biggest crowd. A blind or clever opening pulls the wrong people and hides the ad from the right ones.
- Spend the most time here. The same ad with a different opening can swing results many times over [Stated].
- Test openings to learn which benefit most buyers want. Then split the creative mix in that proportion. Keep a minority appeal if it pays.

### Offer service, don't ask for the sale
- Readers care about themselves. Lead with what they gain.
- The best ads don't ask anyone to buy. They give wanted information and make trying it the natural next step.
- People can be coaxed, not driven.

### Be specific
- General claims and superlatives roll off and make the reader discount everything else. A specific claim reads as either true or a lie, so it gets taken at face value.
- Tell the process behind the product, step by step. The first one to tell it owns it, even if everyone in the field does the same thing.
- A specific claim takes no more room than a vague one.
- Measure before you claim. A false specific is a boomerang.

### Tell the full story
- Once there's attention, use every claim that's proven to matter. One fact wins one reader, another wins another.
- Write for the first-time reader. The people already sold aren't reading.
- Length follows the size of the decision: a line for a small purchase, the whole story for a big one.
- Crowded, wordy ads are usually the measured ones that won.

### Samples
- The product itself, met after the story has been told, is the best salesperson. A sample is often the cheapest way to win a buyer.
- Give it to people who've heard the story and asked for it. A cold handout cheapens it.
- Don't charge a token fee for the sample; the small charge cuts response hard [Stated].
- Make responding easy, capture the contact, and follow up on the *same argument* that brought them in.

### Show the bright side
- Picture what people wish to be, not what they are now. Show the result, not the problem.
- Tell people what to do, not what to avoid. Assume the yes.
- No attacks on rivals. No before-and-after.

### Psychology he tested
- Curiosity sells.
- Cheapness is a weak appeal; people want a bargain, not to be treated as poor.
- Price signals worth.
- An offer limited to a group the reader belongs to beats a general one.
- A thing the maker pays for beats a thing that's merely "free."
- Tell people what to notice and they'll find it.
- Inviting comparison shows confidence.

### Don't pay to change habits
- Converting people who don't want the category costs too much. Spend on people already leaning that way.
- Pick the claim the most people want. Prevention sells poorly.
- Ride a trend others built. Don't try to build one.

### Research until the one fact turns up
- Read and ask for weeks. One fact can carry a campaign.
- Find the true rival. It's often a habit or doing nothing, not another brand.
- Route every customer comment to the writer. Keep a file of what competitors claim.
- Work out how to win one typical person before going after thousands.

### Individuality
- Put a real, named person behind the ad. A person talking is believed; a company isn't.
- Once a tone wins, hold it. Appeals should always sound like the same heart.
- Stand out by doing good things in a distinct way, not by being odd.

### Pictures
- A picture has to outsell the words it replaces.
- Show the product, or people the reader would like to be. Nothing odd for its own sake.

## The specificity pass (run on every draft)

1. Mark every general line: superlatives, "many," "lots of," "quickly," "proven," "for years."
2. For each, look in the brain for the real fact: a count, a duration, a date, a step, a name. Tag it Stated or Verified with its source.
3. Swap the general line for the fact.
4. If no real fact exists, **cut the line.** Never make one up.
5. Check the process description: can a reader picture what actually happens, step by step?

## What carries to Meta in 2026, and what doesn't

| 1923 | Now |
|---|---|
| Keyed coupons | Purchase events per ad; cost per buyer over click-through or hook rate |
| Town against town | Capped split tests with clean budgets |
| Mailed samples | A free chapter or excerpt; a first lesson; a preview of the event |
| "Never change a winning ad" | Keep the proven message, change the first 3 seconds |
| Small type, no white space | No dead seconds, no filler lines. **Not** small text: see `Advertising to Older Audiences.md` |
| Letters | Email and Messenger follow-ups, continuing the ad's own argument |
| Dealers, distribution, window displays | No use here |

> **Brain override — where Hopkins loses.**
> - **Andromeda wins over "never change a winner."** Every iteration still needs a visibly new first 3 seconds (`Making Iterations.md`).
> - **"Show the bright side" versus agitating failed solutions.** The brain's other docs, and the brand's strongest hook on record, lean on what didn't work. Hopkins says the positive picture wins. Treat this as a test, not a rule: the calm outcome against the failed-solution opening, judged on cost per buyer.
> - **"The opening selects buyers"** is done by topic and by first- or third-person story, never by a second-person statement about the reader's health, mind, money or body.
> - **Price as a sign of worth, offers limited to a group, deadlines:** only with true figures, real limits and real dates.
> - **The invented friendly persona** he describes is not used outside the long-form native format the CEO cleared. The brand has a real founder.
> - **His disease and cure examples** are never swiped, and his line about people being made well by mental impressions is never quoted.
