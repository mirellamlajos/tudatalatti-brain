# Market Awareness and Sophistication

> One of the five Copy Foundations docs. Parker reads it before writing or grading any hook, headline, lead, native ad, script or page. It answers one question: **given what this buyer already wants, already knows and has already been told, what is the opening move?** [[Belief Building in Long Copy]] covers what comes after the opening. `Killer Performance Ads.md` has the short version of the awareness levels; this doc is the full one and wins where they differ.

All numbers and results quoted from the book are the author's claims [Stated].

## The law under everything

Copy can't create desire. Desire is already out there, built by forces bigger than any ad budget. The job is to find the strongest desire the product can serve and point it at the product. An ad that argues people into wanting something new pays badly. An ad that meets a want people already carry pays well.

So the work before the first word is analysis, not phrasing. Three questions set the opening:

1. **Which desire** makes this market?
2. **How much do they know** about what satisfies it? (awareness)
3. **How many similar claims** have they already heard? (sophistication)

## 1. Pick the desire

**Where desires come from.** Permanent forces (instincts like health, looks, belonging; problems nobody has solved) never fade, so the problem there is standing out. Forces of change (trends, what media and other advertisers have taught people to want) move, so the problem there is timing.

**Score each candidate desire on three dimensions:**

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Urgency** | How strongly is it felt right now? A constant ache beats a passing itch. |
| **Staying power** | Does it come back, or is it satisfied once? |
| **Scope** | How many people share it? |

A product usually touches two to four desires. **Only one leads.** Pick the one with the most total force, and let the others show up later in the copy. Schwartz calls this the most important choice in the ad; a wrong pick can't be fixed by good writing.

## 2. Find the functional product

The physical product (pages, chapters, hours, materials) never leads. **What the product does** leads. List every performance the product delivers, group them by the desire each one serves, and lead with the group that matches the chosen desire.

Physical facts earn their place later, five ways: they justify the price, document quality, promise the result lasts, sharpen the mental picture, and (most useful in a crowded market) give an old promise a new reason to be believed.

## 3. Read the awareness state

The opening has one job: stop the right person and get the next line read. It doesn't have to sell, name the product or state the main promise. Which opening works depends on the state. A line that works in one state fails in the next.

| State | The buyer | Opening move |
|---|---|---|
| **Most aware** | Knows the product and wants it | Product name plus the deal: price, gift, ease of ordering |
| **Product-aware** | Knows the product, isn't sold yet | Show the name, then do one of the seven tasks below |
| **Solution-aware** | Knows what they want, not that this product delivers it | Name the want or its solution, prove it can be done, show the mechanism lives in the product |
| **Problem-aware** | Has a need, doesn't link it to the product | Name the need, dramatize it, bring the product in as the natural answer |
| **Unaware** | Doesn't know the desire, won't admit it, or can't put it in words | No price, no product, no promise. An identification opening (below) |

**Seven tasks for the product-aware buyer.** Reinforce the desire · sharpen the picture of how the product satisfies it · extend when and where it satisfies · bring new proof · announce a new mechanism that does it better · announce a mechanism that removes an old limit · change the image or mechanism entirely to get clear of rivals.

**Shapes for the problem-aware opening.** The problem alone · problem plus solution · an old limit removed · a substitute for an unpleasant task · prevention · one emotionally loaded word.

### The unaware market

This is where most of the audience sits and where most ads miss. Signs a market has gone unaware of you: spend goes up and volume goes down, and the product name, the promise and the desire all stop pulling.

**Three elimination rules for the opening:**
1. No price. Price means nothing to someone who doesn't want the thing yet.
2. No product name.
3. No direct statement of what the product does, which desire it meets or which problem it solves.

**What's left is the market itself.** Open by echoing a feeling, an attitude or a dissatisfaction so the right people pick themselves out. The opening sells only the next paragraph. The body then walks the reader from recognition, to seeing the desire or problem, to learning a solution exists, to the product.

**Eight identification patterns:**

| # | Pattern | What the opening does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hidden dream** | Gives words to a private hope the reader has never said out loud |
| 2 | **Hidden fear** | Names a worry the reader carries but doesn't voice |
| 3 | **Accepted image** | Opens on a picture people readily accept, to reach a topic they won't face head-on |
| 4 | **Wordless desire** | Projects a desire through image and mood alone |
| 5 | **Shared resentment** | Voices a grievance the reader already holds. Reaches far more people than the direct promise |
| 6 | **Final triumph** | Shows the moment of winning, not the work that got there |
| 7 | **Result of the problem** | Tells, in the third person, what the problem cost someone, for readers who'd reject the problem if it were named to them |
| 8 | **Result of the accomplishment** | Shows the reward and hides the effort, for readers the effort would scare off |

**Warnings.** This is the easiest opening to miss with. Cute or startling isn't enough. The reader must see themselves in it, and it should turn away as many people as it draws.

## 4. Read the sophistication stage

Sophistication is how many similar promises the market has already heard. It sets how the promise has to be made.

| Stage | The market | The move |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | You're first | State the need or the claim simply and directly. Dramatize, prove |
| **2** | Rivals have arrived, the direct claim still works | Push the working claim to its limit. Runs out of believability fast and invites regulators |
| **3** | They've heard every claim | **A new mechanism.** The emphasis moves from what it does to how. The claim shrinks in the headline and is restated in full below |
| **4** | A rival's mechanism is working | Enlarge the mechanism: easier, faster, surer, fewer limits |
| **5** | They no longer believe the category's advertising | Drop claim and mechanism from the opening. **Identification** with the reader, the same move as the unaware state |

Notes:
- A new mechanism has to be new, legitimate and believable. Each one the market has seen makes the next harder to land.
- A new sub-category resets the clock to stage 1.
- Stage 3 only pays where the market renews itself: a recurring want, an unsolved problem, or a purchase people repeat.
- **How to diagnose the stage:** count the claims and mechanisms in competitors' live ads ([[Analyzing Public Ad Accounts]]) and count "I've tried X" mentions in reviews ([[Customer Review Mining Method]]). Three or more tried solutions per buyer is stage 3 or later.

> **Brain note — how this lines up with the older docs.** `Problem-Solution Headline Writer.md` uses HIGH and LOW sophistication. Read LOW as stages 1–2 and HIGH as stages 3–5, then pick the exact stage here. `Scriptwriting.md` ("Market sophistication → what to emphasize") maps stage 4 to delivery and proof and stage 5 to the root cause. Schwartz's own mapping is the one in the table above: stage 4 enlarges the mechanism, stage 5 moves to identification. Both can be true in one ad (an identification opening, then a fresh root cause in the body). For the **opening**, this table wins.

## Planning notes

- **Three levels of creativity.** Swapping words in a winning ad gives an echo that only works for the same product, market and moment. Formulas help phrasing, not idea-finding. Only analysis of this market finds a new idea.
- **Research is a direction, never a headline.** A finding from reviews or comments tells you where to dig. It still has to be turned into an opening.
- **One trait sums up the product.** Many traits, one that leads.
- **Prevention through loved ones.** People won't picture future trouble hitting themselves. They will picture it touching someone they love.
- **Splinter markets.** A market isn't one piece. Same desire, different reasons for it, different mechanisms. A small advertiser can own one segment, then use that win as the point of difference when going wide.

> **Brain override — the wall filters every example in the book.** Schwartz's examples are mostly health, weight-loss and patent-medicine ads, second-person attribute hooks and one invented "would have sold for" price. Take the structure, never the claim type. Identification openings ship in the first or third person. Numbers need a source in the brain. The compliance framework in `Brand/Compliance and Claims Watchlist.md` outranks anything here.

## What Parker writes down before any opening

Four lines, in the brief or at the top of the working notes:

```
Desire: [the one that leads] — urgency / staying power / scope
Awareness: [state] — evidence: [...]
Sophistication: stage [1–5] — evidence: [...]
Opening move: [from the tables above] — pattern or headline move #[...]
```

If any line can't be filled from the brand context or the data, tag it Data-limited and say what would settle it.
