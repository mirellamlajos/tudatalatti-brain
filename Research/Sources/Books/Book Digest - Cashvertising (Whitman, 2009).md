## 2. Thesis

Advertising has one job: get people to act. Most ads fail because their makers chase cleverness and don't know why people buy. People care about themselves, so an ad works when it ties the product to one of eight hardwired desires, makes the reader run a mental demo of owning it, proves the claim, and then pushes for action while making action easy. Whitman packages this as 17 borrowed psychology principles plus 41 craft techniques from direct response and old readership research (Starch, Caples, Ogilvy, Hopkins, Schwartz). His recurring rules: simple words, benefits over features, specifics over generalities, long copy over short, proof over assertion, a deadline, and test everything.

## 4. Frameworks and methods

### 4.1 Life-Force 8 (pp. 22–25)

Eight desires he says are biological and drive more sales than everything else put together:

1. Staying alive, enjoying life, living longer
2. Enjoying food and drink
3. Freedom from fear, pain and danger
4. Sexual companionship
5. Comfortable living conditions
6. Being superior, winning, keeping up with others
7. Care and protection of loved ones
8. Social approval

Already in the brain: `Killer Performance Ads.md` lists the LF8. What that doc does not have is everything below.

**Nine learned (secondary) wants** (p. 24): being informed; curiosity; cleanliness of body and surroundings; efficiency; convenience; dependability and quality; expression of beauty and style; economy and profit; bargains. He ranks them well below the LF8 because they're learned, not wired.

**Desire formula** (p. 25): an unmet need creates tension, tension creates desire, desire creates action to satisfy it. An LF8 appeal creates the tension.

**Vicarious fulfilment** (pp. 25–26): reading how someone else satisfied a desire is pleasant in itself. The first use of any product happens in the buyer's head, so copy should make the reader picture using it.

**Five-step chapter summary** (pp. 27–28): pick an LF8 want; build the appeal on it; write so the reader mentally demos the product; make the claim believable (proof, guarantee); push for action.

### 4.2 The 17 principles (Ch. 2)

1. **Fear factor** (pp. 30–35). Fear creates stress and stress creates a wish to act. Credits Pratkanis and Aronson, *Age of Propaganda*, for four conditions: the message scares strongly; it gives a specific fix; the fix is seen as effective; the person believes they can do it. Drop one and it fails. Too much fear freezes people. Tap fears that already exist and are widely known; don't invent new ones. Deadlines and scarcity are fear of loss. He says fear is ethical only when the product truly solves the threat.
2. **Ego morphing** (pp. 35–38). Give the product an identity buyers want to wear. People buy to show traits they have or to look like they have them (credits Pratkanis and Aronson; Stec and Bernstein's Balance Theory, 1999). Image does the work; little argument needed. Works best on looks, smarts, money, sexual success.
3. **Transfer** (pp. 39–41). Borrow trust from respected people, institutions and symbols (seals, logos, lab coats). The reader sees a credibility cue and questions the pitch less. Best case is a real endorsement; next best is a recognised symbol. Credits the Institute for Propaganda Analysis.
4. **Bandwagon** (pp. 41–44). People need to belong (he places it third on Maslow's ladder). Three reference groups: aspirational (want to join), associative (share your values), dissociative (don't want to be in). Tie the product to one. For aspirational, the reader must be able to identify with the people shown. Credits Stec and Bernstein's consensus-implies-correctness heuristic and Cialdini.
5. **Means-end chain** (pp. 45–46). People buy for a later payoff, not the thing. Keep asking what the benefit of the benefit is until you hit the core one, then show that end result in copy and images.
6. **Transtheoretical model** (pp. 47–48). Five stages, credited to Prochaska (1994): precontemplation (unaware), contemplation (aware, thinking), preparation (wants details), action (buys), maintenance (habit). Two ways to handle a mixed audience: one ad that covers all five stages, or a series that walks from stage one to five.
7. **Inoculation** (pp. 48–51). Credited to William McGuire. To lock in an existing preference: warn that an attack is coming, give a weak version of it, invite the person to defend. The attack must be weak or it backfires. In ads: tell prospects what rivals will say and what to look out for, so rival claims get read through your filter. He calls it a consumer-advocate stance.
8. **Belief re-ranking** (pp. 51–55). Beliefs are defended like survival. Two routes: shift the focus of a belief by giving a new way to see it (emotion or evidence), or, easier, raise or lower how much an existing belief matters. Strengthen beliefs people already hold with facts and everyday examples. Never tell people they're wrong; show comparisons and let them conclude. He says the prospect should feel the decision was theirs.
9. **Elaboration likelihood model** (pp. 55–58). Two routes to attitude change. Central: logic and deep thought, for costly or personally important buys; feed facts, stats, testimonials, case histories. Peripheral: pleasant cues, images, celebrities, for low-stakes buys. Attitudes formed by the central route last longer and resist rival pitches.
10. **Six weapons of influence** (pp. 58–65). Cialdini's cues, mnemonic CLARCCS. Best for peripheral-route selling.
    - *Comparison*: everyone like you is doing it.
    - *Liking*: people buy from those they like; attractive faces persuade more (credits Down and Lyons, 1991); men look at men and women at women because of identification.
    - *Authority*: official-looking figures cut the need to check.
    - *Reciprocation*: an unasked gift of real value creates a pull to give back. It must be a gift, not something requested or earned.
    - *Commitment/consistency*: the Four Walls method. Ask a chain of questions that each get a yes, so buying is the consistent next step. Weaker in print than in person.
    - *Scarcity*: limits and deadlines; scarcity also signals exclusivity.
11. **Message organization** (pp. 65–66). If they can't follow it, they can't be persuaded. Short section; points to Secret 1.
12. **Examples vs statistics** (pp. 66–68). Vivid examples beat stat lists because they trigger emotion and a mental test drive. Credits Petty and Cacioppo (1986): examples match personal experience and take less effort. Use both; let the product set the mix.
13. **Message sidedness** (pp. 68–70). Two-sided messages persuade more than one-sided, but only if you defend your side and knock the other (credits Allen, 1991). Praise the rival fairly, then say why you're better. Comparison charts work. Also: tell people who should not buy.
14. **Repetition and redundancy** (pp. 70–71). Repetition builds familiarity, then comfort, then trust. Too much causes irritation (Petty and Cacioppo, 1979: three exposures raised agreement, five lowered it). Redundancy means the same message in a new format and wording, so it reads as new.
15. **Rhetorical questions** (pp. 71–73). A statement dressed as a question. He lists studies for and against, and cites a 1998 review (Gayle, Preiss, Allen) finding no real persuasive effect. His one safe conclusion: they may help people remember the message.
16. **Evidence** (pp. 73–75). People buy when they believe the thing is worth more than the money. Evidence is any fact, object or opinion the advertiser did not create (credits Reinhard, 1988). Strong evidence works on deep thinkers and skimmers alike; for skimmers, make it easy to grasp with charts and quotes from respected people.
17. **Heuristics** (pp. 76–79). Mental shortcuts. He covers one: length implies strength (Stec and Bernstein, 1999). Lots of copy, many testimonials, long numbered lists of reasons and many customer photos read as proof by volume, even unread.

### 4.3 The 41 ad-agency secrets (Ch. 3)

1. **Psychology of simplicity** (pp. 81–90). Write so people understand. Use the Flesch Reading Ease score (0–100, higher is easier; 60–70 is 8th–9th grade, 70–80 is 7th, 80–90 is 6th). Four prescriptions: short simple words (70–80% one syllable); short sentences (about 11 words, one thought each); short paragraphs (4–5 sentences) plus one-line question-and-answer paragraphs to pull the eye down; lots of personal pronouns. Also: concrete words; paragraph leaders (several paragraphs opening with the same phrase) for tempo; and a real-time aside that comments on the moment of reading.
2. **Bombard with benefits** (pp. 90–93). A feature is a part; a benefit is what the buyer gets. Readers keep asking what's in it for them. Drill: state a feature, have someone answer with a bored so-what, then reply starting with how the buyer benefits.
3. **Biggest benefit in the headline** (pp. 93–97). Most readers stop at the headline. The headline should also pick its audience. Shorter headlines get read through more often, but content matters more than length. Rule: biggest benefit always; if two are equal, run the shorter.
4. **Crank up the scarcity** (pp. 98–99). No deadline tells people there's no need to buy now. Use a hard deadline (a date) or a soft one (limited quantity, first N callers, limited seats).
5. **22 headline starters** (pp. 99–102). Caples's four headline qualities first: self-interest, news, curiosity, quick easy way. Starters: Free, New, At last, This, Announcing, Warning, Just released, Now, Here's, These, Which of, Finally, Look, Presenting, Introducing, How, Amazing, Do you, Would you, Can you, If you, Starting today. Don't use curiosity that pulls the wrong audience.
6. **12 ways into the body copy** (pp. 102–104). Continue the headline's thought; ask a question; quote an authority; give a free usable tip; challenge the reader to test it; open with your own early skepticism; open with what others say; write it like a news report; go heavy on "you"; tell a dramatic story; give super-detailed specs; open with a very short sentence.
7. **360 degrees** (pp. 104–105). Different gets noticed. Put a print ad inside a circle on a page of rectangles. Credits *Printer's Ink*.
8. **Reverse-type pitfall** (pp. 105–107). Light text on dark is harder and slower to read. Exception: a few large headline words.
9. **Extreme specificity** (pp. 107–110). Say exactly what you do, how, with what, in numbers. Teach buyers the details they don't know. The one who says it wins, even if rivals do the same thing. Works through length implies strength.
10. **Ogilvy layout** (pp. 111–112). Top two-thirds photo, headline under it, copy below with a drop cap, logo bottom right. Reverse version: one-third photo, two-thirds copy. Headline goes under the picture. Always caption pictures.
11. **Psychology of typefaces** (pp. 112–117). Type carries mood. Print body: serif. Screen: sans serif. Headlines in initial caps, not all caps (all caps only for a few words). Long headlines in black on white or yellow. Screen advice: Arial at 12 pt and up, Verdana below that, rarely under 10 pt, Georgia for a formal look, 14 pt for older readers.
12. **Pro design** (pp. 117–118). Don't design your own materials. Ask agencies for freelancer names, or use design students; see samples first.
13. **Power of questions** (pp. 118–119). Questions make readers want the answer and turn a monologue into a talk. He credits the NLP open-loop idea and admits he has seen no research for it.
14. **Granny Rule of direct mail** (pp. 119–127). Mail should look like a personal letter from grandma: plain envelope, handwritten address, real stamp, warm talk. Letter opens with the top-ranked benefit, often as a question, and works in fast and easy. AIDA order. List quality: recency, frequency, dollar amount. Reply form restates the offer, shows what they get, carries guarantee and deadline, and is called a trial certificate, not an order form.
15. **Social proof** (pp. 127–128). Ask customers for testimonials with a short questionnaire. Get a signed release with name and city and give something small in return so the agreement holds.
16. **Guillotine principle** (pp. 128–129). A face looking straight at the reader is the best attention-getter and adds trust. Put your own face in every ad; make the headline a quote from you, in quote marks, next to the photo.
17. **PVAs** (pp. 129–133). Powerful visual adjectives: swap flat wording for concrete, sensory, specific wording so the reader sees the product. The advertiser who describes fully looks more able than rivals who stay silent.
18. **Directing mental movies** (pp. 133–138). Experience is built from five channels: sight, sound, feeling, smell, taste (VAKOG). Facts alone don't sell. Walk the reader through using the product in as many channels as fit. More brain space occupied means more persuasion. This is his reason long copy wins.
19. **Battling inertia** (pp. 138–140). Two steps: make acting easy, then ask for action. Pre-filled forms, many payment and shipping options, payment plans, a guarantee, step-by-step ordering, and the plain statement that ordering is easy.
20. **USP** (pp. 140–144). Without a difference there's no reason to prefer you. Name what you are in a distinct way, then make the business match the name. Tell your story proactively. Position offers too: a specific, unusual person-and-situation headline beats a generic promise. Points to Ries and Trout.
21. **Buy your own island** (p. 145). A half-page island (two columns wide, three-quarters deep) owns the page for less than a full page. Small repeated ads suit two-step selling.
22. **Authority positioning** (pp. 145–148). See yourself as someone with knowledge worth sharing, then publish it everywhere: headshot on everything, short free reports (3–10 pages), a Q&A column ad where you write both questions and answers, talks, workshops, a book, interviews, a blog.
23. **Sales letter as survey** (pp. 148–150). Mail customers a short 1–10 survey and close it with an offer open only to people who answer, framed as a thank-you. Payment goes back in the same envelope. Bonus question: what would you change if you owned my business?
24. **Pictures** (pp. 150–153). Visuals raise noticing and reading. Photos beat drawings. Show the product; better, show it in use, in a real setting, with people. Best-drawing subjects: babies, mothers with babies, groups of adults, animals, sports, celebrities, food. The picture must connect to the message.
25. **Grabbers** (pp. 153–155). Attach a small object (a coin, a dollar bill, a bad sample) to the top of a letter and open by explaining why it's there. Credits Robert Collier, 1937. Test with a 500/500 split.
26. **Long vs short copy** (pp. 155–158). Long, well-written copy outsells short. A reader who is sold early can stop and buy; the one who needs more gets it. Restate each benefit from several angles, to feeling and reason both. He says this holds online.
27. **Offer testing** (pp. 159–160). A failed ad may mean a weak offer, not a dead product. Test framings: two-for-one instead of half off, free add-ons, segment specials, every fifth visit free, a free report. Fix order: headline, then price, then offer.
28. **Survey power** (pp. 160–162). Research before creative. Ask customers what they like, hate, want, would pay. Make answering easy with multiple choice and 1–10 scales; reward answers; prepaid reply.
29. **Editorial energizers** (pp. 163–164). Make the ad look and read like a news story in the publication's type and tone, with quotes from customers. Keep it calm; hype breaks the effect. Offer a free report.
30. **Coupon persuader** (pp. 164–167). A dashed coupon border lifts response even with no discount, because people are trained to act on coupons. He explains it as an NLP anchor, like Pavlov's bell.
31. **Seven online boosters** (pp. 167–169). Email about weekly. Expect click-through from under 1% (rented lists) to over 20% (own list, strong incentive). Test HTML against text. Opens depend on a familiar sender, a personal subject line, and a relevant offer. Bigger and interactive banners do better. Gentle motion lifts clicks. Mystery ads get clicks but few sales.
32. **Multi-page** (pp. 169–170). Several ads in one issue have a more-than-additive effect (Starch). Ranks ten formations, led by three right-hand pages in a row.
33. **Guarantees** (pp. 170–172). A strong guarantee tips the scale between skepticism and the wish to believe. Longer guarantees sell more and get fewer returns, because short ones keep buyers watching the clock. Offer the longest and strongest in the field, show it off with a certificate border and signature, and ask why rivals offer less.
34. **Psychology of size** (pp. 172–174). Attention grows with roughly the square root of ad area. To double attention, quadruple the size.
35. **Page and section position** (pp. 174–175). Front or back, left or right makes almost no difference. The ad matters.
36. **Fantastic Four** (p. 175). Four positions do earn more notice: inside front cover, opposite the contents page, back cover, inside back cover.
37. **Colour preferences** (pp. 176–179). Preference order: blue, red, green, violet, orange, yellow. Blue climbs with age. Best paper: white or yellow; best ink: black, dark blue, red; best pair: black on yellow. Colour pulls more than size.
38. **Psychology of pricing** (pp. 179–181). Odd endings (99, 98, 88, 79) signal value; round numbers signal quality. Pick by positioning.
39. **Psychology of colour** (pp. 181–182). Colour changes perceived weight (dark looks heavier), taste, and category. A colour a rival owns will confuse buyers.
40. **Wrap in white** (p. 183). Buy more space and leave it empty around a smaller ad.
41. **Cleverectomy** (pp. 183–186). Clever is not effective. A headline should say what is sold, to whom, the benefit and the offer, with no puzzle to solve. Test instead of arguing.

### 4.4 Hot lists (Ch. 4, pp. 188–193)

- **22 response superchargers** (pp. 188–189): sell, don't decorate; offer free information; short words and sentences; long copy with the fluff cut; pile on benefits; show the product in action; say "you"; selling subheads and photo captions; visual adjectives; give the full pitch now; always use testimonials; make acting easy; reply coupon; deadline; free gift for fast replies; say order now; free shipping; a bill-me or credit option.
- **9 ways to convey value** (p. 189): announce a sale; coupon; compare the price to a small daily cost; explain why the price is low; break it into a per-day figure; state what it's worth; say what others paid; deadline; psychological pricing.
- **13 ways to make buying easy** (p. 189): full contact details; directions; say it's easy to order; take orders by every channel and payment type; toll-free number; a guarantee longer than rivals'; installments.
- **11 coupon boosters** (p. 190): tell them in the headline to return it; buy-one-get-one instead of half off; a big Free at the top; say and show what the coupon brings; bold border; hard or soft deadline; check-off boxes; label it valuable; room to write; arrows pointing at it.
- **46-point ad checklist** (pp. 190–193), four blocks:
  - *Headline*: biggest benefit; emotional grab; uses a starter; much bigger than body and bold; makes an offer; sounds sure of itself; initial caps; in quote marks.
  - *First sentence*: uses one of the 12 openers; flows from the headline; straight to reader benefit; forces the second sentence; "you" early.
  - *Body*: reader benefit; why you and not a rival; order of attention, interest, desire, proof, action; one product per ad; subheads; believable; respectful; emotional; specific; short everything; three dead-simple action steps; asks for the sale; deadline; bullets for many benefits; testimonials; contact details large; logo; ad keyed for tracking.
  - *Layout*: pro designer; headline broken at natural phrase breaks; one clear focal path; white space; indented paragraphs; few separate elements; relevant art; two typefaces, three at most; a person looking at the reader.

## 7. Dated or risky material

**Print and mail only; does not carry to 2026 Meta ads**
- Secrets 7, 21, 32, 34, 35, 36, 40: circle ads, islands, multi-ad formations, page size and position, white wrap. Pure newspaper and magazine media buying.
- Secret 14 mechanics (envelopes, stamps, reply envelopes, mailing lists), Secret 25 grabbers, coupon borders, fax and cheque ordering.
- Secret 31: email and banner figures from the early 2000s. Treat as history.
- Secret 11 screen advice rests on 72 and 96 dpi screens and 1995–2002 tests. Serif-for-print does not apply to feed creative.
- Reverse-type rule is about body text on paper. Meta captions are white-on-dark by norm; his own exception (few words, large type) is the part that carries.
- Flesch scoring is built for English. US reading-level stats don't transfer to Hungarian readers.
- Price-ending studies are in dollars and cents.
- Soft science: left/right brain, NLP open loops and anchors (he admits no research for open loops), VAKOG. His citations are loose; the same book gets two dates in places.

**Breaks the brand's compliance wall**
- *Fear appeals*: Principle 1 (germs, mites), Secret 18 (the spider passage), the Warning starter. Built on health and safety threats.
- *Second-person attribute hooks*: starters Do you, Can you, Would you, If you; the Four Walls chain; Secret 14's openers; opener 9; the gendered body headlines in Secret 1.
- *Health claims in his samples*: blood pressure, quitting smoking, eyesight, allergy relief. Don't swipe.
- *Fake scarcity*: Secret 4 treats soft deadlines as a phrase to add, true or not; the envelope trick of a made-up same-night deadline (p. 123); the consultant with three slots.
- *Invented proof*: sample headlines with made-up figures (512%, 97%, 325%); borrowed authority from an unknown guild logo or a lab coat (pp. 40–41); free work for local names so you can drop them (p. 37); the Q&A column where you write the questions (p. 147); ads disguised as news (Secret 29) and the play-reporter opener.
- *Value framing*: say what it's worth and what others paid (p. 189). Only with prices that were really charged.
- *Rival baiting*: implying rivals hide something behind a short guarantee (p. 172).
- His line that prospects should not notice the influence (p. 54), and paragraph leaders used to make people feel they learned things they didn't (p. 90).

## 8. Applications for this brand

**Genuinely additive to the brain** (checked by keyword against `Knowledge/Domain Knowledge/`): the nine learned wants, means-end laddering, the five buyer stages, inoculation, belief re-ranking, two-sided messaging, length implies strength, the evidence definition, the 12 copy openers, guarantee and offer mechanics, survey-as-offer, legibility numbers, the 46-point checklist. **Overlaps**: LF8 (in `Killer Performance Ads.md`), awareness stages (Schwartz, already used), social proof and scarcity (several docs), reading level (`Scriptwriting.md` already aims at grade 5), big high-contrast text (`Advertising to Older Audiences.md`), ELM (close to System 1/2).

1. **Desire tagging.** Add the nine learned wants next to LF8 in the Idea Bank. For a book on the subconscious, being informed and curiosity are the honest lead wants; LF8 7 (loved ones) and 8 (approval) are the safe emotional ones. LF8 1 and 3 sit too close to health.
2. **Benefit ladder.** Run the benefit-of-the-benefit drill on each product and stop at a claims-safe end state (nyugodtabb).
3. **Stage map.** Use the five stages as a funnel check: native story ads for the unaware, VSL and landing page for preparation, Book 2 to Book 1 buyers as maintenance.
4. **Inoculation block for VSLs and landing pages.** Pull real objections from the review corpora, state each one fairly and briefly, answer it. Keep it as objection handling, not rival attacks.
5. **Who this is not for.** A short two-sided section on event pages. Honest take-away, no fake limits.
6. **Proof by volume.** A wall of [R] reviews with names and towns, and a numbered list of reasons to come. Count only what's real.
7. **Evidence rule.** His definition (proof is what the advertiser didn't make) matches the brain's sourcing rule. Use it as the test for what counts as proof on a page.
8. **Founder face.** Lajos looking at camera, headline as an [F] verbatim in quote marks, beside the photo. Caption every image on landing pages.
9. **Sensory scenes.** Use the five-channel walk-through for native ads and scripts: what the room at the live event looks and sounds like, what reading the book at the kitchen table feels like. Scenes, not symptom lists.
10. **Specifics.** Real counts only: event hours, seats, chapters, pages, years Lajos has taught.
11. **Openers for native ads.** Best fits: own early skepticism, news-report voice (clearly an ad), a very short first line, a free usable tip, continuing the headline.
12. **Safe starters.** New, Announcing, Introducing, Presenting, Just released (Book 2), At last, Finally, Here's, How, This, These. Drop Warning, Amazing, and the four you-question forms.
13. **Legibility for 50+.** 14 pt body minimum on landing pages, dark text on white, no all-caps headlines beyond a few words, headline under the image, one to two typefaces.
14. **Real deadlines.** Event date and real seat count are hard deadlines. Put them on the ad, the page and the checkout.
15. **Guarantee.** If a refund promise exists, make it long, frame it like a certificate, sign it as Lajos.
16. **Reciprocity and authority.** A free chapter or short report given without an opt-in wall; a recurring Lajos-answers format built from real audience questions.
17. **Post-purchase survey.** Five 1–10 questions plus the if-you-owned-this question, closing with a responder-only Book 2 or ticket offer. Answers feed review mining.
18. **Offer tests.** Bring-a-companion framing against a plain discount for events. Real prices only.
19. **Redundancy.** Same message, new format and first seconds. It backs the Andromeda rule with a fatigue caution (three good, five too many, in one lab study).
20. **Headline lint.** Add the cleverectomy test: can a cold reader tell what's sold, for whom, and the offer?
