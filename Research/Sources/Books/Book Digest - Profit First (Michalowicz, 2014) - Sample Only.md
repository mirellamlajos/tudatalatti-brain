## 2. Thesis

Michalowicz says businesses stay broke because the standard formula, Sales - Expenses = Profit, treats profit as leftovers, and people spend whatever sits in the account. His flip is Sales - Profit = Expenses: take profit out of every deposit first, then run the business on what remains (PDF 24-25, 49). The math is the same. The behavior is not. He borrows four diet principles: use small plates, serve sequentially, remove temptation, and enforce a rhythm (PDF 48-50). In money terms:
- Split each deposit into separate accounts by fixed percentages.
- Fill profit, owner pay and tax before operating expenses. If expenses don't fit in what's left, cut expenses.
- Park the profit and tax money in a second bank so it's hard to touch.
- Do the whole routine on a fixed twice-monthly schedule.

He starts small and raises the percentages a few points per quarter, so nothing has to change in a person's habits overnight. He argues that revenue-chasing ("top line thinking") and "just sell" crisis behavior are what actually kill businesses (PDF 32-39).

## 4. The system, in full (as far as the text goes)

**Accounts.** Four core accounts (PDF 49, 52):
- Profit
- Owner's Pay
- Tax
- Operating Expenses

Setup: keep your existing checking as Operating Expenses. Make Tax and Profit savings accounts, and Owner's Pay a second checking account (PDF 52). Then open two more savings accounts at a different bank, one "no-temptation" Profit and one "no-temptation" Tax. Link them so money can be pulled from your main bank's Profit and Tax accounts, but turn off online viewing and checkbooks (PDF 52). Nickname each account with its percentage, for example OPERATING EXPENSES [45%], OWNER PAY [25%], PROFIT [15%], TAXES [15%] (PDF 88). He calls it a plug-in to your accounting, not a replacement (PDF 51).

The excerpt only previews later account types. Fixed-dollar accounts appear in one example: "Employee Pay ($750)" and "Petty Cash ($50)" (PDF 93-95). The book says the dollar-amount naming is explained in Chapter 9 (PDF 88). A "Vault Account" is teased once (PDF 100). None of these are explained.

**The four principles** (PDF 48-50):
1. *Small plates*: split each deposit into the accounts by preset percentages.
2. *Serve sequentially*: fill Profit, then Owner's Pay, then Tax, and what's left goes to expenses. "No exceptions." If expenses don't fit, do not raid the other accounts. It means you can't afford those expenses and must drop them (PDF 50).
3. *Remove temptation*: keep the savings out of reach with a second bank and no convenience features.
4. *Enforce a rhythm*: pay bills twice a month, not whenever cash piles up.

**Real Revenue** (PDF 57-60, 74). All percentages are applied to Real Revenue, not top-line revenue.
- Real Revenue = top-line revenue for the last 12 full months minus "Material & Subs."
- Material & Subs means materials (not your own labor) and subcontractor costs. Subcontractors are people who work autonomously and for others, and are paid by project fee, commission or hourly rate, not payroll (PDF 58).
- If services are delivered mainly by your own employees, put n/a and Real Revenue equals top line (PDF 59).
- Employees count as operating cost, not cost of goods (PDF 59-60). That is why he says Real Revenue is not the same as gross profit.
- Later he adds: if more than half of what you sell comes from materials (manufacturers, restaurants, retailers), use gross profit as the Real Revenue number (PDF 74).
- His examples: a real-estate firm with $5M revenue and $4M in agent commissions is a $1M business, and a $3M staffing firm paying subs $2.5M is a $500K business (PDF 59).

**Instant Assessment steps** (PDF 57-62). Fifteen steps:
1. Enter top-line revenue for 12 months.
2-4. Enter Material & Subs, or n/a.
5. Compute Real Revenue.
7. Enter actual profit. Only count profit sitting in the bank or paid out as a bonus on top of salary. A "profit" that is neither is not counted (PDF 60).
8. Enter owner's pay, meaning salary-type pay only.
9. Enter taxes paid plus taxes reserved.
10. Enter operating expenses, meaning everything except profit, owner pay, tax and materials/subs.
11. Check that profit + owner pay + tax + opex equals Real Revenue, and that Real Revenue + Material & Subs equals top line.
12. Pick the target percentage from Fig. 4 by Real Revenue band. These are the TAPs (Target Allocation Percentages).
13. Multiply Real Revenue by each percentage to get the target dollars.
14. "Bleed" is actual minus target. Negative means you are under-funding that account. Usually profit, owner pay and tax bleed and operating expense runs high (PDF 62).
15. "The Fix": write increase or decrease next to each line.

Law-firm example (PDF 62-63; the figure itself is missing, so only the commentary survives):
- Profit was short about $118,000 a year, with only $5,000 in the profit account.
- Two owners took $190,000 combined, which should be cut by $67,000.
- Operating expenses ran more than $141,000 too high.

**TAPs table.** [Not in the text.] The book calls it Figure 4 (PDF 61) and the image did not extract, so I cannot reproduce the numbers by band. What the text does give:
- Six columns, A to F. From the examples: $225K is column A, $722K is column C, $40M is column F (PDF 61).
- The band boundaries appear in the narrative (PDF 64-65): under $250K; $250K-$500K; $500K-$1M; $1M-$5M; $5M-$10M; $10M-$50M. Six bands match six columns, so the mapping A to F to these bands is [Inferred].
- Trends by band: owner's pay share drops as the company grows, and operating expense share rises through the $1M-$5M band because systems and people become mandatory (PDF 65).
- Scattered numbers, none of them the table: Rodrigo's $350K business gets 35% for owner pay, about $122,000 (PDF 77-78). A brand-new business starts at 1% profit, 50% owner pay, 15% tax (PDF 68). The leftover for operating expenses is "likely" 40-60% (PDF 82). Example splits appear at PDF 88 (45/25/15/15) and PDF 93 (43/15/30/12, with opex first, then tax, owner pay, profit).
- Do not use any of these as the table.

**How to set your own TAPs** (PDF 73-83):
- *Profit TAP*, three options:
  1. Average net income / revenue over 3-5 years for five public companies in your industry or a similar one.
  2. Your own best percentage year from your tax returns.
  3. Pick from the assessment band.
- *Owner's Pay TAP*: pay yourself the going rate for the work you actually do, meaning "the salary you would have to pay your replacement" (PDF 76). Or add up owners' salaries and multiply by 1.25 for headroom (PDF 76). His warning: don't cut your own salary to make the numbers work (PDF 80).
- *Tax TAP*, four options: your last three years of taxes as a share of Real Revenue; your accountant's YTD estimate; a search of published rates; or just use 35% as the taxable rate (PDF 81-82). Then the "simple math": if operating expenses take 55%, the other 45% is what gets taxed, and 45% x 35% is about 16% (PDF 82-83). This is why the assessment example reserves only about 15%, not 35%.

**Runway rule of thumb** (PDF 75), Stated: profit allocation of 5% gives 3 weeks of operating cash, 12% gives 2 months, 24% gives 5 months if sales stopped entirely. His reason for the non-linear jump is that higher profit share means leaner expenses too. He also warns that 50% profit with 10% operating expenses would attract price cuts from competitors (PDF 75-76).

**CAPs vs TAPs.** The term CAP (current allocation percentage) does not appear in this excerpt. The idea does, without the label: your quarterly allocation percentages are what you run now (PDF 89), and TAPs are the target (PDF 73, 84). The mechanics:
- Start at your historical level for each account plus 1 point. A business that has never had profit starts profit at 1%. A business that pays taxes at 5% starts at 6%. Pay at 20% becomes 21% (PDF 88).
- Each quarter, move closer to the TAPs. The conservative suggestion is about 3 points per quarter, in total or spread across accounts (for example profit 5% to 8%, or tax 11% to 12%, profit 5% to 6% and owner pay 23% to 24%) (PDF 98).
- "Never take a step back." He prefers small steps to a big leap you then reverse (PDF 98). Going to 20% profit on day one is compared to donating five gallons of blood at once (PDF 72).
- He suggests pushing later to 22% profit or higher, or cutting operating expenses down to 15% (PDF 100).

**Day-one steps** (PDF 87-90):
1. Tell your accountant. If they push back, his advice is to get a new accountant (PDF 87).
2. Take today's operating-account balance, subtract outstanding checks and payments, and split what's left by your percentages (PDF 89). Split every new deposit the same way.
3. Cut costs by 10% right away. He wants 3 points for this quarter's step plus cushion for the next two quarterly steps, because cancelled bills take a month or two to clear (PDF 90-91). Method: print 12 months of expenses and all recurring charges. Cancel what isn't needed to run efficiently or keep customers happy. Negotiate every remaining expense except payroll (PDF 91). "A lot more about cutting expenses" is promised in later chapters that are not in the file.
4. Keep a celebration list for your first distribution (PDF 103).

**The 10/25 rhythm** (PDF 91-95). All revenue lands in the operating account. On the 10th and 25th (or the business day before if it's a weekend or holiday):
1. Add up the prior two weeks of deposits.
2. Move those dollars out by percentage.
3. Move fixed-dollar amounts from operating into any fixed accounts.
4. Sweep the full Tax and Profit balances to the no-temptation bank.
5. Pay yourself only your set bi-weekly salary from Owner's Pay and leave the rest.
6. Pay employees from the Employee Pay account.
7. Pay all bills from what remains in Operating.

The worked example: $10,000 in deposits goes to Operating $4,300, Tax $1,500, Owner's Pay $3,000, Profit $1,200. Then $750 and $50 move to Employee Pay and Petty Cash. The text says $50 is left after bills but that does not match $3,500 minus $3,500, so read the example as illustrative. If there isn't enough to pay the bills, he says the business is telling you it can't afford them, and he still wants you doing the rhythm; a later chapter is said to cover the adjustment (PDF 95). Reported benefits: he stopped reacting to each bill, spotted patterns such as 80% of bills falling early in the month, found many small recurring bills to kill, and paid vendors on time (PDF 92-93).

**Quarterly profit distribution** (PDF 95-98). Quarters are calendar quarters unless the fiscal year differs. On the first day of each quarter, take 50% of the profit account as a distribution to equity owners, split by ownership. Leave 50% as reserve. Never send profit back to the company (he rejects "plowback" and "retention" as cover terms). Use it for personal benefit. On the same day, pay estimated quarterly taxes (PDF 98). The profit account serves three purposes: cash reserve, growth metric, profit (PDF 96).

**Year-end and rainy-day fund** (PDF 99-100):
- Finalize taxes. If the tax account falls short, this is the one time you pull from profit for something other than distribution. Then raise the tax percentage and lower profit by the same amount.
- If the tax account has too much, move the excess to profit, and maybe cut the Tax TAP (check with an expert first).
- Build a three-month operating-cash reserve. Beyond it, either invest for growth or fund "the Vault" (not explained).

**The $ / smiley / infinity to-do list** (PDF 101-103). Tag tasks $ (likely to bring in money within 60 days), smiley (for a top client), infinity (a system so others can do it). Priority order runs from all three, down to two of three, then one, then blank last. Meant to push the whole team toward profit, clients and systems.

**Debt approach** [Not in the text]: Chapter 6 "Destroying Debt" is missing. All the text says on debt is that the "just sell" cycle ends in debt (PDF 32) and that leftover-thinking builds debt (PDF 24). The Assessment note says a profit lower than expected may have gone to pay down old debt (PDF 60).

**Pricing, customer selection, employees, advanced accounts, efficiency:** no dedicated treatment in the excerpt. What is there: some revenue costs more than other revenue and you must distinguish (PDF 35); the "top customers" idea of The Pumpkin Plan appears in the to-do list logic (PDF 101); bigger companies "leverage efficiency" (PDF 65); pay for the owner is a function of what work you do (PDF 76-80).

## 8. What bears on marketing decisions [all Inferred from the book's logic]

The book never mentions advertising, ad spend, ROAS, launches or events. The word "marketing" appears only in the names of people thanked and in a to-do-list line calling a happy client "the most powerful form of marketing" (PDF 102). Everything below is my reasoning from the mechanics, not the author's advice.

1. **Where ad spend sits.** By the Assessment definition, Operating Expenses is everything except profit, owner pay, tax and materials/subs (PDF 60). Paid media is not named, so it would sit inside Operating Expenses unless the company chose to treat it like a direct cost. The book gives no rule for which. That choice would change Real Revenue and therefore every percentage. Decide it once, in writing, before running the numbers.
2. **A fixed share caps the ad budget.** The book says the leftover for operating expenses is "likely" 40-60% of Real Revenue (PDF 82), and example splits use 43-45% (PDF 88, 93). Payroll, tools, rent, software and ads all share that pool. So ad spend can only be as large as what's left after those, and it moves with the previous period's deposits, not with a spend target.
3. **Scaling spend is paced by deposits.** Under 10/25, money to spend on ads exists only after each deposit is split. Raising spend ahead of revenue means going into the profit/tax/owner accounts or cutting other expenses, and the book says not to (PDF 50). Scaling therefore follows cash that has already come in, not forecasts. That collides with a common paid-media pattern of spending ahead of returns and waiting for payback.
4. **ROAS targets.** The book has no unit-economics view. If a campaign's break-even ROAS is calculated before the operating allocation, it ignores that 15-30% or more of each deposit is moved out before ad bills are paid. [Inferred] A ROAS target set under this system would be judged against the operating share, not gross revenue. This is my extension. Calculate it before adopting the system.
5. **Cost-cutting pressure.** The 10% cut on day one and the "cancel what's not needed, negotiate the rest" rule (PDF 90-91) apply to every line, including tools, agencies and media. He also says the fix may be to remove a line item, not just find it cheaper (PDF 66). Applied to marketing, that argues for pruning channels and vendors, not only bidding lower.
6. **Launches and events with up-front costs.** The system is built around steady deposits on a rhythm. It has no described way to handle a big cost that comes before a big revenue spike (a live event, a book launch, a creative production). Two elements in the excerpt could matter: the profit account's 3-month reserve, with the excess available for "appropriate capital investments" (PDF 100), and the note that "The Vault" exists (PDF 100). Both are unexplained. Whether the profit-first rules allow spending on a launch out of reserve is not answered here, and the full chapters may say.
7. **Growth vs. profit.** The book's stance is health over size, and it says the way to grow is still more sales (PDF 90). It does not say to stop spending on acquisition. It says profit goes first, then expenses have to fit. For a founder-led brand, the practical read is that a growth push has to be paid for out of the operating share or delayed.
8. **Revenue quality.** He notes that all revenue is not the same and some costs far more in time and money to earn (PDF 35), and that "just sell" pushes you toward the wrong customers (PDF 35). The $ / smiley / infinity list rewards work for top clients and repeatable systems (PDF 101-102). That would support keeping ads pointed at the best-fit buyers, but the book does not say how to pick them.
9. **Benchmarks and counterparties.** The whole list of percentages is a US small-business rule of thumb. It is not a marketing-to-revenue benchmark and should not be used as one.
