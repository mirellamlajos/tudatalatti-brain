# Digest: Scientific Advertising (Claude C. Hopkins, 1923)

Source: the PDF text (1,797 lines, PDF pages 1–61). Page refs below are the `===== PAGE N =====` markers, which match the printed page numbers. Every number in this digest is Hopkins's own claim [Stated], a century old and not verified.

## 1. Edition note

- The file is a modern reprint: title page (p.1), a short foreword by Victor Urbach (p.2, not Hopkins), a table of contents (p.3), then all 21 chapters (pp.4–61). Nothing is missing. No chapter is cut short.
- The text was read in full. It's clean enough to trust, but it carries typos that flip meaning in a few spots. Those are listed in section 9. Where a line looks corrupted, this digest says so and doesn't lean on it.
- No illustrations, no sample ads. Hopkins describes ads; he never shows one.

## 2. Thesis

Advertising is salesmanship in print, and its only job is to make sales at a known cost. Because ads can be keyed and returns traced, every question (headline, picture, length, offer, price, plan) can be settled by a small test instead of by opinion. Do that long enough and the winners harden into principles: talk to one typical buyer, offer service instead of asking for the sale, pick your readers with the headline, be specific, tell the whole story, give interested people a no-risk way to try, and judge everything by cost per customer. Anything done for pride, amusement or decoration is waste, and you should at least know what that waste costs you.

## 3. Chapter-by-chapter digest

**Ch 1. How Advertising Laws Are Established (pp.4–7)**
- Principles come from keyed ads and traced returns, mostly coupons. Compare one way against many, record it, and when one method always wins it becomes a rule.
- Where direct response isn't possible, compare one town against another.
- Reply counts aren't the final word. One ad brings junk replies, another good ones. Judge by "cost per customer or cost per dollar of sale" (quote, p.5).
- Some findings only hold in one product line; others are universal. The book claims to cover the universal ones.
- What stays uncertain is people and products, not method. Individuality is essential; imitation is a fault (p.7).

**Ch 2. Just Salesmanship (pp.8–10)**
- Treat the ad as a salesman. Make it justify its cost. It isn't there for "general effect" or to keep a name in front of people.
- The test question: "Would it help a salesman sell the goods?" (quote, p.9). Slogans, clever lines, loud type and odd styling fail that test.
- Fine writing and a showy style hurt. They pull attention off the subject and make people feel sold.
- No length limit. The only readers are interested people; give them enough to act.
- Write to one typical buyer, not the mass. Don't amuse, don't boast, don't show off.
- Go sell in person before writing. Hopkins cites admen who spent weeks selling door to door and interviewing hundreds of buyers.
- The top cause of failure is selling what people don't want. Second is ads written to please the seller.

**Ch 3. Offer Service (pp.11–12)**
- Readers care about themselves, not your profit. "The best ads ask no one to buy" (quote, p.11). They give wanted information, name the user's gain, and offer a trial without cost or risk.
- Examples: a brush maker's 2,000 canvassers open with a free brush, take your pick. A coffee wagon firm in about 500 cities leaves a half-pound to try, comes back, and offers a kitchen utensil paid off at five cents credit per pound bought. An electric sewing-machine motor goes out for a week's free use with a man to show how it works; about nine in ten trials became sales. Cigars: "Smoke ten, then keep them or return them."
- "People can be coaxed but not driven" (quote, p.12).

**Ch 4. Mail Order Advertising: What It Teaches (pp.13–15)**
- Mail order is the honest school because cost and result show at once. Judgment is wrong "often nine times in ten" (quote, p.13).
- Example: a $5 article. One ad pulled replies at 85 cents, a "better" one at $14.20, a third at 41 cents for two years. Untracked advertisers pay 2 to 35 times what they need to.
- What long-running mail order ads look like: small type, no empty space, no borders, no boasting, no entertainment, a coupon as a reminder (people who decide to act forget within five minutes), pictures that sell and are sized by importance, and a complete story.
- "The more you tell the more you sell" (quote, p.14). Bigger ads pay in proportion only when the extra space is filled with selling.
- Incubator example: adding a row of chicken silhouettes grew the ad 50 percent and added zero sales.
- Mead Cycle Company: years of comparisons, ads then left alone; Mr. Mead wouldn't change a word for $10,000.
- You may spend on pride if you like, but know what it costs. When results drop, go back to the mail order model and cut waste.

**Ch 5. Headlines (pp.16–18)**
- The headline's job is to pick out the people you can interest, like calling "Hey there, Bill Jones" in a crowd (quote, p.16). It isn't to draw the biggest crowd.
- Blind or clever headlines pull the wrong people and hide the ad from the right ones. Headlines work like news headlines: they "conceal or reveal an interest."
- People skip three-fourths of what they pay to read. They decide at a glance from headline or picture.
- Hopkins spent more time on headlines than copy, often hours on one, throwing out scores.
- Same ad, different headline: returns swing five- or tenfold. He cites keyed results on nearly 2,000 headlines for one product.
- Test headlines to learn which benefit most buyers want, then split ads among appeals in that proportion. A weaker appeal can still pay. Overlapping audiences get different ads because different people answer different approaches.
- Examples: soap headline "Keep Clean" or "No animal fat" is weak, beauty is strong. A car ad about the universal joint falls flat; "The Sportiest of Sport Bodies" might outpull it fifty to one.
- "The appeals we like best will rarely prove best" (quote, p.18).

**Ch 6. Psychology (pp.19–22)**
- Human nature doesn't change, so these lessons keep.
- Curiosity sells: Puffed Wheat and Rice failed until "Foods shot from guns" and "125 million steam explosions."
- Cheapness is a weak appeal. People want bargains, not to be treated as poor.
- People judge worth by price: the $750,000 painting nobody looked at until they read the price; a $1,000 Easter hat; stating "as a fact" that $100,000 was paid for a formula.
- Guarantees have gone stale. A warrant signed by the local dealer (a neighbor) beat one from a far-off maker.
- "Pay in a week if you like them" beat "money back if you don't" (the two-horses story, p.20).
- Personalization: a set of books sold by the hundreds of thousands once the buyer's name went on in gilt. A leather book "with your name on it, waiting" got nearly everyone to send in the requested information.
- An offer limited to a class (veterans, lodge members, executives) beats a general offer.
- "Try our rivals' too" fixed a substitution problem where "beware of substitutes" had failed.
- A package the maker pays for beats a package given away. Free cheapens; the maker buying it for you adds respect.
- A sample asked for after reading the story lands far better than one handed out cold.
- Tell people what to notice and they find it.
- A signed, motherly persona who wrote to customers like a friend beat a bare business offer next door. A follow-on credit letter, "recommended by Mrs. ___", worked through flattery.

**Ch 7. Being Specific (pp.23–25)**
- Generalities and superlatives "roll off." They make readers discount everything else. A specific claim is either true or a lie, so it's taken at face value.
- Examples: tungsten lamp gives "three and one-third times the light"; "prices reduced 25 percent"; "Our net profit is 3 percent" replaced "Lowest prices in America"; a carmaker's "Our profit is 9 percent" with $735 of hidden costs listed on a $1,500 car; shaving soap that "softens the beard in one minute," holds lather ten minutes, and came from "testing and comparing 130 formulas"; the "78-second shave"; "Used by the peoples of 52 nations."
- The beer case: every brewer said "pure." One described the plate-glass cooling room, the wood-pulp filter, bottles washed four times, a 4,000-foot well, 1,018 yeast experiments. Every brewer did the same things. He was first to tell it, and won.
- A specific claim takes no more room than a vague one.

**Ch 8. Tell Your Full Story (pp.26–28)**
- Once you have attention, use every claim that tests have shown matters. One fact wins one reader, another fact wins another.
- Don't run one claim per ad or serial ads. People read once and decide.
- Write only to new prospects. Current users don't read your ads.
- A finished ad can run for years; returns come from new readers. The sameness only bores the advertiser.
- Length depends on the product: a sentence for chewing gum, columns for a car. The car buyer chose a Marmon because it told him everything; pricier makes ran short ads and lost him.
- "Brief ads are never keyed" (quote, p.27). Don't copy untracked advertisers.

**Ch 9. Art in Advertising (pp.29–31)**
- Pictures cost space, often a third to half of the budget. Use one only when it sells better than the same space in type.
- Don't use pictures to decorate, amuse or grab general attention. No eccentric pictures; they steal from the headline and lower respect.
- Pictures that work: the product itself; people the buyer envies in settings the buyer wants (Arrow Collars, correspondence schools); the Puffed Grains shown large because they raise curiosity.
- Open questions he leaves to testing: costly art versus plain (minor), repeating a picture (probably fine, since readers are new), color (usually no gain, except food).
- These are small economies next to basic principles.

**Ch 10. Things Too Costly (pp.32–34)**
- Changing habits costs too much. Don't spend even part of an ad educating non-users. Toothbrush converts tested at $20–$25 each. Oatmeal education returned nothing measurable.
- Building category demand only pays for combined groups (orange and raisin growers).
- Ride a trend others created; don't try to create it.
- Know lifetime revenue per customer before you buy one. A one-time product may never pay back.
- Pick the claim the most people want. Prevention sells poorly; a toothpaste's beauty appeal beats decay. A narrow claim means paying to reach a hundred readers for one buyer.
- A minority claim can still earn a share of headlines and a line in every ad, set by traced returns.

**Ch 11. Information (pp.35–36)**
- Read and research for weeks; one fact can carry the campaign. The decaf coffee campaign turned on one article in a thousand. "Genius is the art of taking pains" (quote, p.35).
- Examples: 130 men interviewing consumers; 12,000 physicians written to; 1,000 men asked what they want in a shaving soap.
- Pork and beans: a canvass showed only 4 percent bought canned; 96 percent baked at home. The real rival was home baking, not other brands.
- Collect every competitor's literature and claims. Route every customer and dealer comment to the writer. Know yearly spend per user and what share of readers can want the product.
- Verify claims before using them; a false one is a "boomerang." Get exact figures (425 calories per pint, equal to six eggs). Have an authority check every ad on technical lines.

**Ch 12. Strategy (pp.37–39)**
- Settle the foundations first: name, price, competition, substitution, dealers, distribution.
- Price: volume at low margin usually wins; small-use products need margin; in some lines a higher price is itself a draw.
- Figure out how to win one typical user of a rival product before going after thousands.
- "There is nearly always something impressive which others have not told" (quote, p.38).
- Own the category name early (Vaseline, Kodak) or others share the demand you built.

**Ch 13. Use of Samples (pp.40–43)**
- The product, plus the impression you've built around it, is the best salesman. Samples are usually the cheapest way to get customers, however costly they look.
- What a sample does: lets you say "Free," gets action now, gives you a name and address to follow up, and keys the ad.
- Don't charge for samples. Asking a dime may add 40 cents to $1 per reply. Half-price first offers don't pay either.
- Make replying easy. Mail inquiries cost 70 cents; store-redeemed ones 18–22 cents. Offered mail, phone or store, 70 percent phoned.
- Give samples only to people who've read the story and made some effort. Cold handouts cheapen the product.
- Cheating is small. "Only one sample to a home" is enough.
- Don't skimp on the follow-up with the half-sold. Judge by cost per customer.

**Ch 14. Getting Distribution (pp.44–46)**
- Don't advertise what people can't buy. Build distribution town by town, then go national.
- Tactics: name stocked dealers in ads, make coupons redeemable at any store, mail dealers a proof of the coupon ad in advance. Palmolive and Puffed Grains got distribution this way.
- Avoid gift stock to every dealer and consignment. Almost fully dated (see section 7).

**Ch 15. Test Campaigns (pp.47–50)**
- "Almost any question can be answered, cheaply, quickly and finally, by a test campaign" (quote, p.47). The buyers are the court of last resort.
- Test in four or five towns. Learn cost per customer, sale per customer, repeat rate, and how fast cost comes back. "We let the thousands decide what the millions will do" (quote, p.47).
- A test runs $3,000–$5,000 and mostly pays itself back.
- Test product changes too: one new form got near-total disapproval; a later form got 91 percent approval. About $1,000 each.
- Keep testing on winners without disturbing the proven plan. Over fifty plans in five years for one food account; the last cut selling cost 75 percent.
- Settle agency and method arguments with a side-by-side test.

**Ch 16. Leaning on Dealers (pp.51–52)**
- Dealers won't push your brand. Discounts, free cases and displays mostly move sales between stores.
- A sale without a convinced buyer doesn't stick.
- The goal: "buy new customers at a price which pays a profit" (quote, p.52). Every wasted dollar is a customer lost.

**Ch 17. Individuality (pp.53–54)**
- Stand out by doing good things in a distinct way, not by being odd.
- Put a real person in the ad. Signed ads carry personal authority; "a man is talking," not a "soulless corporation" (quote, p.53).
- Fit the persona to the audience: rugged and honest, good fellow, or authority.
- Once a tone wins, never change it. A new writer plays the part like an actor. Appeals must seem to come from the heart, "the same heart always."

**Ch 18. Negative Advertising (p.55)**
- Never attack rivals.
- Show the bright side: the result, not the problem. "Picture what others wish to be, not what they may be now" (quote).
- Tell people what to do, not what to avoid. Assume they'll act: "Send now for this sample," not "Why do you neglect this offer?"
- Positive ads outpulled negative four to one in his tests. "Before and after" ads are "follies of the past."

**Ch 19. Letter Writing (pp.56–57)**
- A letter needs a headline that shows at a glance what's in it for the reader.
- Test letters like ads: 25 versions to 1,000 people each before mailing millions.
- Fancy stationery and postage class change nothing. The content is the appeal.
- Follow up on the same argument that created the inquiry. Don't switch to a new guess.
- Get action now: an inducement, a stated cost of delay, or a dated limit. Example: a card enclosed with a catalog promising a gift with the order; a premium good for one week that moved hundreds of thousands of six-package orders.

**Ch 20. A Name That Helps (pp.58–59)**
- Best: a name that tells the story (Cream of Wheat, Minute Tapioca, 3-in-One Oil, Holeproof).
- Meaningless coined names (Kodak, Lux) are protectable, but most never gain meaning.
- Ingredient or descriptive-generic names (Toasted Corn Flakes, Malted Milk) let rivals share the demand.
- Avoid frivolous names on serious products. If the product needs a common name, add a person's name.

**Ch 21. Good Business (pp.60–61)**
- The mill-wheel versus turbine image: same stream, many times the power.
- Most big advertisers don't know if their ads pay. One spent $700,000 a year and couldn't say. Hopkins's dare: offer $5 to anyone who read the ad through and count the replies.
- Ads that look crowded and wordy are usually the keyed ones that won.
- Tracked, accountable advertising will push out "bunk."

## 4. Principles list (deduplicated)

**Testing and measurement**
- Key every ad; trust no untracked ad, yours or anyone's.
- Judge by cost per customer and cost per dollar of sale, not by reply or click counts.
- Test small first; let thousands predict millions.
- Know payback time and yearly value per customer before scaling.
- Keep testing new plans beside the proven one; don't disturb the winner.
- Settle arguments with a test, not a meeting.
- Your favorite appeal will usually lose. Expect to be wrong.
- If you spend for pride, know the price.

**Salesmanship**
- Ask: would this help a salesman face to face?
- Write to one typical buyer.
- No fine writing, no entertainment, no boasting, no loudness.
- Sell it in person, or talk to buyers, before writing.

**Service over selling**
- Lead with the buyer's gain. Don't ask for the sale; make buying the natural next step.
- Remove risk: trial first, pay later, easy return.
- Coax, don't drive.

**Headlines**
- The headline selects the audience; it doesn't chase the crowd.
- No blind or clever headlines.
- Spend the most time here. Test many.
- Split the ad mix by how each appeal tests; keep minority appeals that pay.

**Specificity**
- Replace every superlative with an exact figure or fact.
- Tell the process behind the product, even if rivals do the same; first to tell owns it.
- Get real numbers measured before you claim them.

**Full story**
- Put every proven claim in every ad.
- Write for the first-time reader only.
- Length is set by the size of the decision.
- A winning complete ad can run for years.

**Offer and samples**
- A sample or trial is the cheapest way to buy a customer when given to interested people.
- Make people ask for it after hearing the story.
- Don't charge a token fee for the trial.
- Make responding as easy as possible.
- A thing the maker pays for beats a thing that's just "free."
- Capture the contact and follow up fast, on the same argument.
- Use real deadlines to beat delay.

**Psychology**
- Curiosity, stated price as a sign of worth, personalization, offers limited to a class, a nearby guarantor, confidence shown by inviting comparison.
- Tell people what to look for and they'll find it.

**Art**
- A picture must outsell the type it replaces.
- Show the product, or people the buyer wants to be.
- Nothing eccentric. Art cost and color are minor.

**Positive framing**
- Show the wished-for state, not the problem.
- No attacks. No before-and-after. Assume the yes.

**Strategy and cost**
- Don't pay to change habits or educate non-believers.
- Pick the claim the most people want; don't lead with prevention.
- Research until you find the one fact.
- Find your true rival (it may be a habit, not a brand).
- Learn to win one person first.
- Own the name of what you create.

**Individuality**
- Put a real, named person behind the ad.
- Hold one tone for good.

## 7. Dated or risky material

**Dated, with today's translation**
- Coupons and keyed ads → pixel/CAPI events, UTMs, per-ad purchase and lead data. The rule "judge by cost per customer, not replies" maps to CPA and new-customer cost over CTR, hook rate or cheap leads.
- Town-against-town tests → geo holdouts, or campaign-level split tests with clean budgets.
- "Never change a winning ad" and "repeat the picture" → the principle (keep the proven message) holds; the practice doesn't. Meta creative fatigue and Andromeda need fresh first-three-second variants. Keep the message, change the opening visual.
- One ad per magazine → creative diversity across angles and formats.
- Small type, no white space, 8-point → print economics. Translates to: no dead seconds, no filler frames, every line sells. It does not mean small text for 50+ and 65+ readers; `Advertising to Older Audiences.md` wins there.
- "Color rarely pays," art-cost debates → drop.
- Mailed samples and store coupons → free chapter, free first lesson, free webinar or event preview, low-friction lead form, Messenger reply.
- "Seventy percent phoned" → offer the easiest reply channel for older buyers (phone, Messenger, one-field form).
- Dealers, jobbers, loading offers, window displays, distribution (Ch 14, 16, most of 12) → no direct use. Nearest analog: don't pay partners, affiliates or bookshops for "push" without measuring net new buyers.
- Letter writing → email and Messenger follow-up sequences; the rules carry whole.
- Patent and trademark naming advice → legal context is 1923 US. Principle only.
- "Entertainment never sells" → too absolute for a feed where the ad has to earn the stop. Keep the test: does the hook select buyers or just viewers?
- Gender and class assumptions (women want beauty "because of men," "poor girls," "simple people") → drop.

**Would break this brand's compliance rules**
- Disease and cure examples used as selling claims: the product that "prevents disease" (p.17), soap that "may tend to cure eczema," the asthma relief (p.34), "people will do much to cure trouble" (p.33). These teach picking cure over prevention as the appeal. Not usable. The safe lesson is only "lead with the appeal most people want."
- "If people can be made sick or well by mental impressions" (p.21). Sits close to the brand's subject. Never quote it in ads or pair it with the method.
- "Hey there, Bill Jones" read literally becomes a second-person attribute callout. Select the audience by topic, first-person or third-person story, not „Szorongsz?".
- "Picture what others wish to be," envy imagery, "before and after" → he already rejects before/after, which fits. Aspirational images must not imply a health result. "Nyugodtabb" is the outcome word.
- Price-as-worth ("we paid $100,000 for that formula," "as a fact") → only with a true, documented figure. No invented anchor or "was" price.
- Class-limited offers and dated deadlines (pp.20, 57) → only real limits: real seat counts, real dates.
- "Created personality" and the signed Mrs. ___ persona → the brand has a real founder; use him. No invented personas, no invented flattery letters.
- "We must have a seeming advantage" (p.38) → must be a true, sourced fact the brand is first to tell.
- Personalization examples that imply knowledge of the reader → keep to opted-in data (name on a certificate or signed copy).

## 8. Applications for this brand

What's additive to the existing docs is the principle layer, not tactics:

1. **One scoreboard.** Add Hopkins's rule to account analysis: an ad is judged by cost per buyer (book order, event ticket), never by lead volume or engagement alone. Cheap leads that don't buy are his "worthless replies."
2. **Thousands before millions.** Before scaling a new angle, event city or Book 2 offer, run a small capped test and record cost per buyer and payback time. Write the result to `Knowledge/Interpreted Data.md` as Verified.
3. **Headline as selector.** Add one check to `Hooks.md` and the headline writer: does this hook pick out likely buyers (people 50+ interested in the subconscious, Lajos's books, a calmer life) or just stop everyone? Do it by topic and story, not second-person attributes.
4. **Appeal mix by test.** Tag each ad with its lead appeal, then set the share of new creative per appeal by measured cost per buyer. Keep minority appeals that pay.
5. **Specificity pass.** Before shipping, swap every general line for a real, sourced figure: years Lajos has taught, number of events held, books sold, pages, chapters, hours in a live day. Each tagged Stated or Verified. If there's no real number, cut the line; don't invent one.
6. **Tell the process first.** The beer lesson fits a method brand: describe step by step what happens at a live event or how a chapter exercise works. Process detail, no outcome claims.
7. **Full story for the big decision.** A live event ticket is the brand's "Marmon." Native ads, VSLs and landing pages should carry every proven claim, each time, for a first-time reader. Supports the long storytelling format already in use.
8. **Sample-first offer.** Test a free first chapter or short free session from Lajos as the entry, asked for after the story, with contact capture and fast follow-up on the same angle the ad used. Don't charge a token fee. Measure cost per buyer against straight-to-sale.
9. **Easiest reply.** For 65+, test phone callback or Messenger against the web form.
10. **Risk reversal.** Test "try first" framings the brand can truly honor (real refund or guarantee terms), and a guarantee voiced by Lajos in person rather than by "the company."
11. **Founder individuality.** Signed, first-person ads from Lajos in one fixed tone. Lock the tone in `Spoken-Script Voice.md`; iterations change the opening visual, not the voice.
12. **Positive picture.** Show the calmer ("nyugodtabb") state and people the buyer would like to be, not suffering. This lines up with both Hopkins and the compliance wall.
13. **Don't buy converts.** Spend on people already open to self-development and the subconscious. No ad time arguing skeptics into the category.
14. **True rival.** Use review mining to find what buyers do instead (nothing, other books, other teachers) and write to win one such person.
15. **Follow-up rules.** Email and Messenger sequences continue the argument that got the click, carry a clear subject line, and use only real dates.
16. **Information habit.** Route every comment and review to the writer; keep a competitor claims file (GetHookd). Both already exist as workflows; Hopkins gives the reason.

## 9. Gaps

- No sample ads, no data tables, no sources. Every result is an anecdote with brands mostly unnamed.
- No test method detail: no sample sizes, no guard against chance, no word on how long to run.
- Nothing on video, sound, motion, feeds, algorithms, fatigue, or retargeting.
- Nothing on testimonials or customer stories as proof. The book barely mentions them.
- Nothing on older buyers, on services, events or information products.
- His "laws never change" stance leaves no room for a feed that punishes repeats.
- Transcription errors that change meaning (flagged, not corrected from memory):
  - p.6: "The perils have increased many fold" contradicts the paragraph around it.
  - p.8: "Any studies done that attempt to sell..." is garbled.
  - p.9: dressy ads are "all right to a certain degree, but is quite important" reads backward.
  - p.15: "court of least resort" (p.47 has "last resort").
  - p.17: "multiply returns from five or ten times over."
  - p.32: "results are traced but unknown" likely should be untraced.
  - p.33: the "$42.50" figure.
  - p.37: "Names that tell stores."
  - p.41: "With a key to guide you, your returns are very apt to cost you twice what they need" should logically be "Without."
  - p.43: "there is no prediction for them."
- The foreword (p.2) is a modern add-on and makes claims about Hopkins ("pioneering test marketing, couponing...") that the book text itself doesn't document.
