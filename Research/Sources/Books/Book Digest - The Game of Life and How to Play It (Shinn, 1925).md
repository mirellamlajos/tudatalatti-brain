## 2. Thesis

Life isn't a battle. It's a game of giving and receiving, and the rules are the spiritual laws in the Bible. What you send out in thought, word and deed comes back. The subconscious mind is power without direction. It carries out whatever you feel deeply or picture clearly, so the way to change your life is to change what you picture and say. Fear is "inverted faith," the one real enemy. You beat it by acting with fearless faith, giving up resistance, sending out good will, and following intuitive "leads." Each person has a "Divine Design," a perfect life plan held in the superconscious. The goal is the "square of life": health, wealth, love and perfect self-expression. Shinn teaches all of this through short "a woman came to me" stories, each ending in a spoken affirmation.

## 3. Chapter-by-chapter digest

**1. The Game (pp. 7–14).** Life is a game of "Giving and Receiving." "Whatsoever a man soweth" (7). The imagination is the "Scissors of the Mind" (8). She sets out the three-part mind (subconscious, conscious, superconscious) and the Divine Design (9). Anecdotes: a man who feared a rare disease, pictured it, and died of it (7–8); a child who play-acted a widow and later became one (8–9); a woman who wanted man "A. B." and was given a better match by the law of substitution (9–10); women who "spoke themselves into a trunk" and toward the poorhouse with careless jokes (11); the woman with eight dollars and a hunch (11–12); the Easter rose-tree (13); a man cured of fear by a sign, "Why worry, it will probably never happen" (13–14). Key terms: imaging faculty, divine selection, law of substitution, "speak the word." She ends with a spoken statement to "smash and demolish" bad subconscious "records" (14).

**2. The Law of Prosperity (pp. 15–21).** God is man's supply, and the spoken word releases it. The rule is to act as if you've already received (active faith). Anecdotes: a woman facing a $3,000 lawsuit told to order an expensive lunch, then paid via a cousin (15–17); a man who planned his excuses for an unpaid debt (17); three kings digging ditches in the desert (17); a woman buying blankets before finding an apartment (17–18); a man who raised $50,000 fifteen minutes before a bank closed (20–21). Key terms: "dig your ditches," demonstration, "Spirit is never too late," "one with God is a majority." Also: doubts rise before a big result ("darkest before the dawn," 18); a friend can hold the vision for you (21).

**3. The Power of the Word (pp. 22–29).** Words make personal "laws." Anecdotes: the man who always misses the streetcar and his daughter who always catches it (22); "lucky monkeys" versus a horseshoe (22–23); a friend afraid of ladders whose ladder vanished when she was willing to walk under it (24); the "old-fashioned chat" full of loss and sickness (25). Then the health claims: disease "has a mental correspondence" (25–26); a stage actress with an "incurable" skin disease "healed completely" after three nights of silence and forgiving a man (26–27); criticism causes rheumatism, jealousy causes "false growths," unforgiveness hardens arteries (27); the "poisoned oyster" woman who quarrelled with nineteen people (27). Then a salesman who blessed his rival and won the sale (28). Ends on good will as protection (28–29).

**4. The Law of Nonresistance (pp. 30–38).** Resist nothing and nothing can resist you; water is the model (30). Evil is the illusion of "two powers." Children mirror parents' fears (31). "Baptize" every event "success" (31–32). Anecdotes: a woman who blessed a man who made her feel poor (32); the phone she "resisted" off the hook, fixed when she blessed the situation (33–34); a woman who ran from her mother and married her (34); a student who lied and "cured" herself after living with a worse liar (35). Also: live in the now, not the past (35), the Christmas-seals story (35–36), begin the day with an affirmation (36–37), and the "wonderful work, wonderful way, wonderful service, wonderful pay" rhyme with two student versions and a $41,000 deal (37–38). Ends: "His barns should be full" (38).

**5. The Law of Karma and the Law of Forgiveness (pp. 39–47).** Life is "a game of boomerangs." Karma is "Comeback." Anecdotes: a friend's "No more talk, I wish to eat in peace" coming back at her (39); a woman who pictured living in a neighbor's house, then he died, then her husband died (41–42); a mother's fear about her daughter's trip dissolved once she "let go" (42–43); the counterfeit $20 from sending "stage money" as a joke, fixed by forgiveness (43–44); a messy house and a "burnt match in the pin-cushion" (44); the $500 fur-lined overcoat that "paid" a $10,000 commission (44–45); a stolen Thanksgiving-dinner sum (45); a toy bought and the same money found on the street (45–46). Teaching: spend and give fearlessly, hoarding leads to loss. Grace (forgiveness) sits above karma (46). She closes with the claim that death could be overcome by impressing the subconscious with eternal life (47).

**6. Casting the Burden (pp. 48–55).** The main technique. Head knowledge won't reach the subconscious, so hand the burden to the superconscious, repeating "I cast this burden on the Christ within, and I go free!" (48–50). Anecdotes: the woman in need of money, an avalanche of supply (49); the two pianos (49–50); resentment lifted (50); a wife who set a place at the table daily for her estranged husband and he returned after a year (51–52); the cracked plate as "signs of land" (52); making believe, and the poor woman who spent on a hat and married a rich man (53). Fear: "walk up to the lion" (53–54). Ends with the germ claim and Revelation (54–55). Music and dance impress the subconscious (52).

**7. Love (pp. 56–65).** Real love is selfless and fearless; jealousy is its worst enemy. Anecdotes: "The Cap," who married the woman after she could say "God bless the Cap wherever he is" (56–58); a wife indifferent to marriage whose husband left (59); a man told he's "a two" by numerology (59–60); artists who despise money (60); the film "Greed" (61); a wealthy woman with 67 necklaces whose arms paralyzed (61–62); a student who lost her resentment (62); the "marble woman" employer with an angel inside (62–63); a mother "so happy it wasn't respectable" (63–64); a woman whose mid-night prayer saved her brother in a mine explosion (64). Key ideas: "I salute the Divinity in you" (57), money as "God in manifestation" that must circulate (60), "All disease, all unhappiness, come from the violation of the law of love" (62).

**8. Intuition or Guidance (pp. 66–74).** Speak the word, then wait for a "lead" (a hunch, a chance remark, a line in a book). Anecdotes: giving $100 on a lead (67); tithing (67); "I could smell Palm Beach" (68); the pointless bakery trip (68–69); the woman whose "congestion" of stored furniture caused bodily congestion (69); the friend who condemned an "other woman" and later fell for a married man (70); the horoscope man (71); the $2,000 loss recouped through a landlord (72–74). Warnings against psychic guidance, numerology and horoscopes (71). Stated remedies for indecision (71).

**9. Perfect Self-Expression or The Divine Design (pp. 75–84).** Each person has a unique destiny. Demand it: "let the genius within me now be released" (75). The man with seven cents who gave her one (76); a boy's exam, where he "spoke the word" for arithmetic only (78); the trip abroad where she relied on God until she got home (78–79); Robert Fulton's tea kettle (79). "Poise is Power" (80). Anger, fear and worry as "deadly sins" (80). "Walk up to the lion" again (80–81). Unfinished sewing linked to a stalled demonstration (81–82). Being a good receiver (82–83). Reincarnation as the reason some are born rich and healthy, others poor and sick (83). Ends on man freed from karma, sin and death (84).

**10. Denials and Affirmations (pp. 85–95).** Word demands exactly: "the divine selection," "under grace, in a perfect way" (85–86). The $1,000 that arrived as injury indemnity (85); the student limited to $600 (86); the French gold-nugget-that-was-brass legend (86–87). Pray as command and thanks, not begging (87). The pink paper-cutter as "active faith" (88); the man who broke a bookcase pane and "supplied himself with oxygen" (88); "I'll take nothing less" (89); eyeglasses lost through impatient desire (89); the mended umbrella (90); a phone "treatment" for an ill stranger, with instant effect in Europe (90–91); the $100 bill in the safe-deposit box (92). Visualizing versus "visioning" (91). The power of the name of Jesus Christ (92). Closing: "fearless faith, nonresistance and love" (93). Pages 94–95 give a list of affirmations grouped by purpose (prosperity, faith, right conditions, health, eyesight, guidance, hearing, work, freedom).

## 4. The belief system as a model (her teaching, stated plainly)

- **Three minds (8–9).** The subconscious is "power, without direction," like steam or electricity. It has "no power of induction," so it just carries out. The conscious mind is the "mortal or carnal mind." It sees sickness, death, poverty and limitation and impresses them on the subconscious. The superconscious is "the God Mind within each man," the realm of perfect ideas.
- **Impressing the subconscious.** Whatever you "feel deeply or image clearly" gets "carried out in minutest detail" (8). It is a "silent listener" and like a phonograph record (14). Means she names: spoken words, repeated affirmation, acting as if (buying blankets, seals, an overcoat), making believe (53), music and dance (52), giving thanks in advance, and casting the burden. She says the subconscious "has no sense of humor" (11).
- **The spoken word.** Words are "a tremendous vibratory force" (15). "By thy words thou shalt be justified" (22). Idle jokes and complaints are commands. She says you only dare use words to "heal, bless or prosper" (25).
- **Imagination.** The "imaging faculty" is creative. What you image "sooner or later externalizes" (7). Image only good. Later she prefers "visioning" (intuitive) over forced "visualizing" (77, 91).
- **Fear.** Fear is "inverted faith" (13), "misdirected energy" to be turned into faith (53). Fear attracts the thing feared (42). The cure is to "walk up to the lion" (54).
- **Casting the burden (48–51).** A burden is an adverse thought rooted in the subconscious. The superconscious "fights man's battle," so you hand it over by repeating the statement, sometimes for hours.
- **Nonresistance (30–34).** There is only one power. Evil is an illusion from "soul sleep." Resisting keeps a situation with you. Bless it, "agree with thine adversary," and it falls away. She says this is not being a door-mat (33).
- **Karma and forgiveness (39–47).** You get back what you give. Knowledge raises responsibility (40). Grace and forgiveness can "rub out" mistakes (44) and transcend karma (46). Forcing results by personal will brings "ill-got" gains (42).
- **Intuition (66–74).** Speak the word, then ask for a definite lead and don't act until you get one. Never violate a hunch (12).
- **Divine Design (9, 75–77).** A perfect plan exists for each life. Ask for "the divine selection" (the right job, home or partner), not the specific thing you want.
- **Affirmations and denials (85–91).** Deny the appearance, then always follow with an affirmation. Word it completely ("wonderful service for wonderful pay," 38). State it as already received. Don't plead. Change wording to suit the person (37).

## 5. How the book persuades

**The core pattern.** Short principle, scripture line, then an anecdote ("A woman came to me…", "I knew a man who…", "For example:"), then a payoff line, then an affirmation to say aloud. She uses "For example:" as a tic, over 40 times.

**Story shapes:**
1. *Problem, wrong thinking spotted, fix, result.* She asks "why did you attract it?" and the person finds the cause (stage money, the trunk, "I wish I could get rid of my glasses").
2. *Deadline squeeze.* A named deadline (Saturday, the 15th, noon on Friday), doubt, then a last-minute reversal with a small odd detail (the cousin with the long white beard, the train fifteen minutes late).
3. *Small concrete act of faith.* Tinsel twine, blankets, a paper-cutter, a fur coat. The small purchase is the "bridge."
4. *Reversal by coincidence.* The ladder that's gone, the landlord's "divine mistake," the umbrella mender at the corner.
5. *Punchline testimony.* "I married my mother," "so happy it wasn't respectable," "Who's the matter with you?"

**Tone and rhythm.** Brisk, chatty, first-person, with humor ("burnt match in the pin-cushion," "lucky monkeys," "sixty-seven necklaces"). Sentences are short and declarative. She uses commands ("Speak the word," "Dig your ditches"), short paragraphs, and rhymes for memory ("a rhyme sinks easily into the subconscious," 37). Emmet Fox's blurb (98) describes her as "colloquial, informal, friendly, and humorous," using "everyday examples."

**Authority.** Scripture is quoted constantly, but reinterpreted: "Lord" means "law" (40), "image" means "imagination" (26), the Bible is "a book dealing with the science of the mind" (77). She also cites Plato, Ouspensky, Maeterlinck, Whitman, the Greeks ("Know Thyself"), unnamed "metaphysicians," "a keen observer," and "science" as a general noun. The book never names an experiment or study.

**Reader moves.** She predicts doubt ("darkest before the dawn"), so a failure to get results reads as proof the method is working (18, 51, 89). She defends against the "door-mat" objection (33). She says knowledge without practice "suffers greatly" (40). She makes the reader responsible for every event (72, 89).

**Recurring metaphors.** Game and cards ("take every trick," "never miss a trick"), boomerang, mirror, bank ("Bank of the Universal"), electricity and steam, record and phonograph, scissors, lion, ditches, seeds and harvest, engine or channel, dynamite, blueprints and the Master Architect.

## 8. Risk notes

**Compliance breakers if copied into ads:**
- **Disease caused by thought or emotion.** "Every disease is caused by a mind not at ease" (27). Criticism causes rheumatism, unforgiveness hardens arteries, jealousy causes "false growths," anger "poisons the blood" (27, 80). Fear of a disease brings it, and the man who pictured a rare disease "died" (7–8). Mothers' fear "attracts" children's diseases (31, 64). All of these blame the sick person or their parents. Never use.
- **Cure by thought or word.** The skin disease "healed completely" (27); "instantaneous healing" (69); phone treatment of a stranger (90–91); "many healings" (92); a fearless person is "immune" to germs (54–55); death could be "overcome" (47). These are direct cure/heal/prevent/immunity claims.
- **Health affirmations.** "Divine Love floods my consciousness with health, and every cell in my body is filled with light" and the eyesight/hearing affirmations (94–95) are symptom-and-cure framing.
- **Guaranteed money results.** Almost every money story ends in a windfall that arrives on time (the $3,000, $50,000, $10,000, $41,000 stories). The book says "I have never known [tithing] to fail" (67) and "sooner or later comes the manifestation" (50). She also tells readers to spend money they don't have (the $500 coat, the expensive lunch, the paper-cutter, the toy). That is harmful for a 50+ audience on fixed incomes.
- **Blame framing.** If it doesn't work, the reader "doubted" or "resisted" (18, 89). Every mishap, including a theft (the Thanksgiving money, 45) or a lost debt, is traced to the victim's thoughts or fear.
- **Second-person attribute hooks.** She addresses "you" constantly ("you poisoned the oyster," "you are hating him"). Direct lifting of those lines would break the no-second-person-attribute rule.
- **Composite-style anecdotes.** "A woman I know" stories are unnamed, unsourced and unverifiable. Under the brain's tags they are [C] composites at best. None can be used as a testimonial or "a customer said."
- **Death-by-thought.** The house story (41–42) implies a woman's desire caused two deaths. The widow story (8–9) and the mining-brother story (64) are similar.
- **Faith-based claims.** Many claims need Christian framing ("the Christ within," "in the name of Jesus Christ," 92). A secular Hungarian brand shouldn't borrow the religious authority.

**Dated or possibly offensive material:**
- "A colored student" with rendered dialect (p. 89). Period racial language and phonetic dialect.
- "Man" and "he" as the generic human throughout, and gender roles (a woman's perfect self-expression "may be in becoming a perfect wife… not necessarily a public career," 76–77; wives as "an inspiration to her husband," 59).
- Reincarnation as the reason some are "born poor and sick" (83). This reads as blaming the poor and disabled.
- Numerology and horoscopes dismissed; "psychic plane" seen as dangerous (71), while she also treats "lucky" objects as fine if you "recognize the power behind."
- Terms like "the lucky monkeys," "stage-fright" and period detail (victrola, film "Greed," "yellow bills") mark it as 1925.
- Mother-blame ("mothers often unconsciously attract illness… to their children," 31) will hurt.
- She calls her methods "scientific" ("How scientific it then is, to cast the burden," 49; "the scientific reason," 13). These are not scientific claims, and the book cites no study.

## 9. What a modern brand in this category could take from it safely

- **Structure.** A short principle, then one small, specific, human scene, then one line the reader can say aloud. Keep the single-line "punchline" and the rhymed or rhythmic phrase, because she is right that rhymes stick. Use founder verbatim [F] or real customer comments [R] in place of her unnamed "women."
- **Tone.** Warm, humorous, conversational, first-person, plain words. This matches the brain's neighbor-over-the-fence voice.
- **Themes that don't need a claim.** Words you say to yourself shape your mood and choices; careless jokes and complaints become habits; noticing the "old records" you replay; letting go of a fight instead of forcing; not living in the past; starting the morning with a chosen sentence; giving without fear; "salute the good in the other person"; wait for a nudge, then act; a unique place only you can fill (framed as personal meaning, not destiny).
- **Metaphors that carry over.** The phonograph "record" (swap "subconscious" for the brand's own terms), the lion you walk up to, "dig your ditches" as preparing before proof, the "signs of land," the "old-fashioned chat" versus "new-fashioned chat," the gold nugget legend (a reminder that belief changes behavior; skip the wealth promise).
- **Calm-word language.** Her "poise," "nonresistance" and "peace" map to "nyugodtabb," the claims-safe outcome word. Use the calmer-not-cured framing.
- **What to use only as internal tone reference.** All anecdotes. They are [C]-grade at best.

## 10. Gaps
