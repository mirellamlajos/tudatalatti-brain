## 2. Thesis

Copy can't create desire. Desire already sits in the market, built by forces bigger than any ad budget. The writer's job is to find the strongest existing desire a product can serve, meet the prospect at the exact point of what they already know (awareness) and what they've already been told (sophistication), and channel that desire onto one product. That gives the headline, which only has to get the next sentence read. Body copy then does three jobs at once: builds the desire, gives the product a role the buyer wants to play, and builds belief step by step so the claim is accepted before it's argued. No formula works twice, because every product-market-timing mix is new. The method is analysis: questions, not templates.

## 3. Chapter outline

- **Foreword, Preface (pp. 2–8).** Schwartz reframes the book as market-making, not only ad-writing. People don't change; the direction of their desires does. The job is "building larger mice," meaning a bigger felt need.
- **Introduction (pp. 9–12, truncated).** He's a mail-order writer paid on measured results. Formulas are answers to old problems; analysis handles new ones.
- **Ch. 1, Mass Desire (pp. 19–28).** What mass desire is, where it comes from, its three dimensions, and the three-step job. Splits every product into the physical product and the functional product.
- **Ch. 2, State of Awareness (pp. 29–52).** The headline's one job. Five awareness states, each with its own headline move. Most space goes to the unaware market: three "don't" rules and eight worked patterns.
- **Ch. 3, Market Sophistication (pp. 53–66).** Five stages set by how many similar claims the market has heard, and the move for each. Cigarettes run through all five, twice.
- **Ch. 4, 38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline (pp. 67–74).** "Verbalization": phrasing options once the idea is fixed.
- **Ch. 5, Creative Planning (pp. 75–84).** Three levels of creativity, motivation research, product personality, prevention headlines, splinter markets, recap.
- **Ch. 6, Inside Your Prospect's Mind (pp. 86–90).** Desires, identifications, beliefs. Copy length depends on how much each one needs.
- **Ch. 7, Intensification (pp. 91–120).** Thirteen ways to show one promise from fresh angles, with long mail-order samples. Ends with how to do it across a campaign.
- **Ch. 8, Identification (pp. 121–142).** People buy roles. Character and achievement roles, the product's "primary image," and how to build on it. Limits on what a market will believe.
- **Ch. 9, Gradualization (pp. 143–165).** Belief depends on structure. Line-by-line teardown of the TV repair manual ad, then a list of belief devices.
- **Ch. 10, Redefinition (pp. 166–177).** Removing built-in objections: simplification, escalation, price reduction.
- **Ch. 11, Mechanization (pp. 178–186).** Answering "how does it work?" at three depths. Price cuts need a mechanism too.
- **Ch. 12, Concentration (pp. 187–196).** Showing the other ways to meet the desire fall short, while yours doesn't. Two structures.
- **Ch. 13, Camouflage (pp. 197–206).** Borrowing the trust readers give their media: format, phrasing, and two anti-"ad voice" moods.
- **Ch. 14, The Final Touches (pp. 207–237).** Verification, reinforcement, interweaving, sensitivity (four sample ads), momentum, mood.
- **Epilogue (pp. 238–239).** Reading list: Watkins, Hopkins, Kennedy, Lasker, Collier, Ogilvy, Reeves, Biow, Campbell, Caples and others.

## 4. Frameworks and methods

### 4.1 Mass desire and its three dimensions (pp. 19–24)

**What it is.** He defines it as "the public spread of a private want." A market is born when enough people privately want the same thing to make selling to them pay. Ads can only channel it; running against it sinks even big budgets (his cases: Chrysler's short car in 1948, Ford selling safety in the horsepower years, the Edsel).

**Sources.**
- *Permanent forces:* mass instinct (looks, virility, health) and unsolved technical problems. The desire never fades, so the writer's problem is standing out: fresh appeal, new believability, a new mechanism.
- *Forces of change:* trends and style; mass education (media, social pressure, the sum of all advertising). The writer's problem is timing and being first.

**Three dimensions to score a desire on.**
1. **Urgency / intensity** (constant pain vs. a small headache).
2. **Staying power / repetition**: can it be sated? (raw hunger vs. a taste for fine food).
3. **Scope**: how many people share it.

Pick the desire with the most total force. A product touches two to four desires, but only one can lead the headline. He calls this the most important choice in the ad.

**Three-step job.** (1) Choose the desire. (2) Acknowledge it, reinforce it, or offer the way to satisfy it, in one headline statement. (3) Show how the product's performances satisfy it.

### 4.2 Physical vs. functional product (pp. 25–28)

The physical product (materials, build) never makes a headline. The functional product (what it does) sells. List every performance, group them by the desire each serves, lead with one. His car example has seven: transportation, dependability, economy, power, recognition, value, novelty.

Physical facts work later in the ad, five ways: justify price; document quality; promise the performance lasts; sharpen the mental picture; and, most important in crowded fields, give an old claim a new basis for belief (new mechanism, new quality, or freedom from an old limit). That last is the "point of difference."

### 4.3 What sets the headline (pp. 30–31, 53)

Three questions: what mass desire makes this market; how much do they know about how the product satisfies it (awareness); how many similar products have they been told about (sophistication).

The headline's job is to stop the prospect and get sentence two read. It needn't sell, name the product, or name the main appeal. Only a most-aware market with a price deal lets the headline do the whole sale.

### 4.4 Five states of awareness (pp. 31–52)

Each is walled off from the next. A headline that works in one fails in another, and dies when the market moves on. (Brain terms: his state 3 ≈ "solution-aware," state 4 ≈ "problem-aware.")

**1. Most aware** (p. 32). Knows it, wants it. Headline = product name plus price, gift or convenience.

**2. Knows the product, doesn't want it yet** (pp. 32–35). Show the name; use the headline for one of seven tasks:
- (a) reinforce desire, by association, example, sensory sharpening or illustration;
- (b) sharpen the picture of how it satisfies;
- (c) extend where and when it satisfies (Coca-Cola in winter);
- (d) bring new proof;
- (e) announce a new mechanism that does it better;
- (f) announce a new mechanism that removes an old limit;
- (g) change the image or mechanism entirely to escape rivals (the sophistication problem).

**3. Knows the desire, not that a product exists** (pp. 35–37). Name the desire and/or its solution in the headline; prove it can be done; show the mechanism lives in your product. Needs analysis, a feel for trends, and the knack of naming what nobody has named.

**4. Has a need, doesn't link it to the product** (pp. 37–39). Name the need and/or solution; dramatize the need; bring in the product as the natural answer. Sub-patterns: problem alone; problem plus solution; removal of an old limit; all three; substitute for an unpleasant task; prevention; one emotionally loaded word; a verbal twist when the plain promise is stale.

**5. Completely unaware** (pp. 39–52). The prospect doesn't know the desire, won't admit it, can't word it, or the product has fallen out of favor. Tell-tale signs: volume falls as spend rises, and the name, the function and the desire all stop pulling.

*Three elimination rules:* no price in the headline; no product name (easy on the logo); no direct statement of what it does, which desire it meets, or which problem it solves.

*What's left is the market.* Write an identification headline: echo a feeling, attitude or dissatisfaction so the right people pick themselves out. It sells only the next paragraph. The body walks them from recognition, to awareness of the desire or problem, to knowing a solution exists, to the product.

*Eight patterns shown:*
1. Give words to a hidden dream (correspondence school; p. 43).
2. Exploit a hidden fear (Postum; pp. 43–44).
3. Open on an accepted image to reach a topic people won't take head-on (Odorono; p. 45).
4. Project a desire that can't be said in words, by image alone (Marlboro; pp. 45–46).
5. Use a shared resentment to reach far more people than the direct promise would (TV repair manual; pp. 46–47).
6. Project a final triumph the prospect identifies with (the piano ad; pp. 48–49).
7. Project the *result* of a problem for people who'd reject the problem named outright (Listerine; pp. 49–50).
8. Project the *result* of an accomplishment for people scared off by the work (the pay-raise ad; p. 50).

*Warnings* (p. 51): easiest headline to miss with. It can't be merely cute or startling. The prospect must see themselves in it, and it should turn away as many people as it draws.

### 4.5 Five stages of market sophistication (pp. 53–66)

| Stage | Market | Move | His example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | You're first | Simple and direct: name the need or claim, dramatize, prove | First weight-loss headline |
| 2 | Direct claim still works; rivals arrive | Take the working claim to its limit | Roses to mums to 17,000 blooms; horsepower race |
| 3 | They've heard every claim | **New mechanism** in the headline; emphasis moves from *what* to *how*; claim shrinks to a word, then is restated in full below | "Floats fat out"; "wonder drug" |
| 4 | A rival's mechanism is working | Enlarge the mechanism: easier, faster, surer, fewer limits | "No-diet" wonder drug |
| 5 | They no longer believe the category's advertising | Drop claim and mechanism; **identification** with the prospect (same as awareness 5) | Postum; Marlboro |

- Ways to be first (p. 54): technical breakthrough; new market for an old product (Ovaltine); untapped medium (Revlon on early TV); unnoticed performance (Lifebuoy).
- Stages 2 and 4 both run out of believability. Stage 2 also draws regulators (pp. 56–57, 61).
- Stage 3 pays only where the market renews itself: recurring instinct, unsolved technical problem, or a purchase people redo (p. 57).
- A new mechanism must be new, legitimate, and accepted as believable. Each prior one makes the next harder (p. 61).
- Cigarettes run all five (pp. 62–63). Filters then restart at stage 1 after the cancer scare and run all five again (pp. 64–65). A new sub-category resets the clock.

### 4.6 Verbalization: 38 ways to strengthen a headline (pp. 67–74)

Use after the idea is set, to strengthen the claim, freshen it, or pull the reader into the body. If you're first, the bare claim is best.

1. Measure the claim's size (filter-trap count; pounds; blooms).
2. Measure its speed (aspirin dissolving in two seconds).
3. Compare it (six times whiter; $300 less than rivals).
4. Metaphor ("banishes" corns; "melts" fat).
5. Make it sensory (tastes just-picked).
6. Demonstrate with a prime example (boxer can't flatten the paper cup; the Rolls-Royce clock).
7. Dramatize the claim or its result (the pay raise; the piano).
8. State it as a paradox (the bald barber; win by picking losers).
9. Remove limits (without surgery; without dusty odors).
10. Tie it to admired people or values (athlete; decorators).
11. Show in detail how much work it does (all five stomach troubles).
12. Ask it as a question (who else wants…?).
13. Offer information on how to get it (How to…).
14. Tie authority to it (the boss mechanic; what doctors do).
15. Before and after (the Coldene pair).
16. Stress newness (Announcing; Now).
17. Stress exclusivity (ours alone; only X has…).
18. Make it a challenge (which twin?; does she or doesn't she?).
19. Case-history quotation (the no-cavities line).
20. Condense: swap your product with what it replaces (a ring job in a tube).
21. Symbolize with a parallel reality (the Atlantic gets one-fifth as long).
22. Connect mechanism to claim (feeds fumes back into the engine).
23. Contradict how they think the mechanism works (the golf pro's right-hand advice).
24. Connect need and claim (one answer to an advertising problem: find the man).
25. Offer information in the ad itself (Why Men Crack; what everybody ought to know about stocks).
26. Turn the claim or need into a case history (Aunt Meg; the chicken-salad order).
27. Name the problem ("day-time fatigue").
28. Warn of pitfalls without it (don't invest a cent until…).
29. Emphasize by phrasing: split in two or repeat ("nobody but nobody").
30. Show ease with a bar everyone clears (if you can count to eleven).
31. State the difference (what separates premium gasolines).
32. Surprise them that old limits are gone (crush it: nothing happens).
33. Address those who *can't* buy (already had your vacation? don't read this).
34. Address the prospect directly (to the man who wants the presidency).
35. Dramatize how hard it was to make (they had to invent a new candle).
36. Accuse the claim of being too good (is it immoral to earn this easily?).
37. Challenge limiting beliefs (you're twice as smart as you think).
38. Question and answer (the United Delco ad).

### 4.7 Planning notes (ch. 5, pp. 75–84)

- **Three levels of creativity.** Word substitution makes "echo ads" that only work for the same product, market and moment. Formulas help phrasing, not idea-finding. Analysis is the only route to a new idea.
- **Motivation research.** A source of hidden desires, taboos and market splits, and a way to test hunches. A finding is a direction, never a headline. Failed ads raise "why" questions that become research questions.
- **Product personality.** Many traits; one sums it up and leads (Cadillac: quality).
- **Prevention headlines.** People won't picture future trouble hitting themselves. They will picture it hitting loved ones (toothpaste, life insurance).
- **Splinter markets.** A market isn't one piece (weight loss for looks vs. health). Same appeal, different mechanisms. A small advertiser can own a segment, then use that win as its point of difference when going wide.

### 4.8 Three dimensions of the mind (pp. 86–90)

**Desires**, **identifications** (roles the buyer wants to show), **beliefs** (their picture of how the world works). Copy length = what's needed to build desire, plus fit the product to their self-image, plus get past existing beliefs. Advertising can't argue with beliefs; it starts from them.

### 4.9 Intensification: 13 ways (pp. 91–115)

**Rule:** you can't repeat, but you can reinforce. Each fresh setting for the same promise adds weight. Stop when an angle isn't different enough. Test: re-read the ad days later.

1. **Present the claim directly**, in full detail (rose ad, slide projector, model jet; pp. 94–97).
2. **Put it in action**: show how it does it (pp. 97–99).
3. **Bring in the reader**: their first day owning it (pp. 99–100).
4. **Show how to test it**: reader proves it to themselves (spark-plug idle test; one-evening memory test; pp. 100–101).
5. **Stretch benefits over time**, week by week (plant food; pp. 101–102).
6. **Bring in an audience**: celebrities by testimonial, ordinary people by story or case history (p. 103).
7. **Show experts approving**: their surprise is the draw (pp. 104–105).
8. **Compare and contrast** old and new (pp. 105–106).
9. **Paint the black side**: open the wound, then the fix (pp. 106–107).
10. **Show how easy it is**: tiny effort, big payoff (p. 108).
11. **Metaphor, analogy, imagination**: the English course pictured as the teacher at your elbow (pp. 108–109).
12. **Summarize with a catalog**, one line per benefit. *Horizontal* widens (more uses, more buyers); *vertical* deepens one desire. The "shotgun" after the "rifle" (pp. 109–113).
13. **Make the guarantee the climax**: restate each promise as a guarantee term (pp. 113–114).

**Across a campaign** (pp. 115–120). Find one central idea and restate it in fresh disguises. Four models: fixed line, changing opening demo (Colgate's "invisible shield": only the first ten seconds vary); fixed headline, changing pictures, "Rule and Example" (Toni, Clairol); a fixed focal trait with no fixed words (Cadillac); the product as focus, a new fact per ad (Volkswagen). He names the **"Creative Gamble"**: a headline with no product or claim, staked on winning the read.

### 4.10 Identification (pp. 121–142)

**What it is.** A second kind of desire: for expression and recognition. The product becomes a tool for reaching a role, or a badge that it's been won.

**Two kinds of role.** *Character roles* (adjectives: charming, well-read) are private, unspoken and untested, so they're easier to accept than performance claims, mostly through pictures. *Achievement roles* (titles: executive, homeowner) are invisible until shown through things owned. Roles he says most Americans buy (p. 131): virile or feminine; adventurous; liked; important; modern; above all successful.

**Method.**
1. Find which roles the market accepts for this product, which it rejects, which pulls hardest.
2. Find the **primary image** the product already has.
3. If it's good, intensify it several ways at once (Marlboro: virile men, virile settings, the tattoo).
4. If it's neutral or bad, keep it, turn it down, and use it as a bridge to better images (Chesterfield kept the man smoking and led the eye to the woman).
5. Every added image must grow out of the primary one.

**Limits** (pp. 138–139). Two tests: does the market believe the product has these traits, and can they see themselves in them, now or one step up? If not, open on an image they accept and bridge. Failure case: a deodorant soap sold to the mass market with high-society images; the disbelief spread to the performance claims.

**Image sources in the product** (pp. 139–142): appearance, components, technical background (Micrin mouthwash in a lab-style bottle).

### 4.11 Gradualization (pp. 143–165)

**What it is.** Order the ad so each statement is accepted before the next is made. A claim's strength = its content + the preparation before it. He redefines awareness as *readiness to accept* (p. 148), so the strongest claim isn't always the best headline. His fix: test a smaller, believable headline and bridge to the same big claim.

**TV repair manual teardown** (pp. 149–157).
1. Headline voices a suspicion owners already hold. No claim.
2. An inclusion question nearly all answer yes to.
3. First promise, tied on with "Then…", wrapped in familiar troubles. The *how* stays vague because the reader isn't ready for "do it yourself."
4. More symptom questions: a run of yeses.
5. A bold statement (most breakdowns are needless), backed at once by manufacturer test stories.
6. "If your set were cared for like these…" logic repeats the early promises, now with proof.
7. The risky new idea (you can do it) slips in as one more item in the series: same paragraph shape, "And, most important," the echoed "these experts have discovered."
8. Repairs recast as minor adjustments.
9. Payoff: you pay a repairman for knowledge; have it and you don't pay.

**Devices** (pp. 159–165).
1. **Inclusion question** for early yeses.
2. **Detailed identification**: list their problems so closely they know you've been there.
3. **Contradiction of present beliefs**: needs strong authority; it loosens, it doesn't convince.
4. **Language of logic**: "the reason is," "here's why," "therefore," "in case after case."
5. **Syllogistic chain**: each step forces the next (bigger spark, stronger explosion, more power).
6. **Other forms**: if/then; echoed proof phrases; promise–proof–promise; paragraph parallelism.

### 4.12 Redefinition (pp. 166–177)

Give the product a new definition so an objection never forms. Simplest form is the flip: the liability becomes the proof (Lifebuoy's smell).

1. **Simplification** (too hard). Rename the hard thing (repairs become adjustments, breakdowns become warning signs); tie it to something familiar (the TV to the body). The English course shrinks its subject to small counted pieces: 69 words, a dozen rules, 25 errors, 15 minutes a day. The shift from the reader's world to the product is "the turn" (p. 168).
2. **Escalation** (not important enough). Widen the benefit (good English becomes the power to win people over); show something big hangs on it (a cheap part robbing an expensive car); show it's in play every waking hour.
3. **Price reduction** (too dear). Change what the price is compared with. His sample stacks about ten bargain cues in two paragraphs: hand-made, a higher would-be price stated twice, "pays for itself," a condition to qualify, "only."

### 4.13 Mechanization (pp. 178–186)

Readers make three demands as they go: tell me more; who says so; **how does it work?** The question is never whether to include a mechanism, only how much.

- **Name it** when the market already knows it. Then compete on price or features.
- **Describe it** when they don't. Promise, then reason-why (Rinso, 1926). Mechanism copy isn't a lecture; it sells a second claim that proves the first.
- **Feature it** in the headline when the market is sophisticated and promises sound alike.
- **Price cuts need one too** (pp. 184–186). Collier's shirt letter explains the mill's overstock for six paragraphs before the bargain.

### 4.14 Concentration (pp. 187–196)

Five ways to beat rivals: better product; stronger, wider, more believable promise; product role; out-reacting them; direct attack. This is the last. Rule: never attack a weakness without giving the fix in the same breath.

- **Use** when your budget is smaller and prospects are the rival's customers. **Skip** when you lead.
- **Structure A:** bad, good, bad, good in parallel sentences, each with a because (pp. 190–192).
- **Structure B:** what happens now with the old way, told as the reader's own experience, then what happens with the new (pp. 193–195).
- Can shrink to one "without…" phrase.

### 4.15 Camouflage (pp. 197–206)

Readers trust their media, and in time its look and phrasing. Borrow that by:
1. **Format**: match the host's type, subheads and picture style, even if ugly.
2. **Phraseology**: news cues (dateline, byline); mail that always gets opened; the newscaster's sound.
3. **Mood against "ad voice"**: *understatement* (short, plain, few adjectives, self-kidding: Volkswagen) and *deadly sincerity* (state the flaws and who it isn't for, so the benefits are believed).

### 4.16 Final touches (pp. 207–237)

- **Verification.** Proof hits hardest when the reader is already asking for it. Place it there, not in a proof box. *Documentation* = outside facts; *mechanization* = proof by logic; *verification* = where documentation sits.
- **Reinforcement.** Two ideas joined right beat their sum (Avis: second, so we try harder). An admitted flaw just before a claim makes it land.
- **Interweaving.** Each sentence carries promise, image and logic together.
- **Sensitivity.** Be writer and reader at once; shift copy type when the reader has had enough. "A new structure is a new ad."
- **Momentum.** Forward phrases ("Here's how," "And that's only the beginning") and open loops (raise interest, raise a question, imply the answer comes later).
- **Mood.** Word choice and rhythm set emotion unnoticed. If everyone notices the emotion words, the ad is bad. Read it aloud.

## 6. Author's claims and numbers (all [Stated])

- Copy can't create desire, only channel it (p. 19).
- Channeling existing desire can return $50–$100 per $1; educating returns at best $1 per $1 (p. 21; figures read through OCR noise).
- One desire leads the ad; a wrong pick can't be saved (p. 24).
- The headline is about 10–20% of an ad's space and shouldn't carry the whole sale (p. 30).
- Most advertising speaks to the product-aware state (p. 33).
- A headline that works at one awareness stage fails at another (p. 40).
- An unaware headline should reject as many people as it attracts (p. 51).
- Late in stage 2, readers discount claims by about half (p. 56).
- The five or ten headline words are about 90% of the ad's value (p. 83).
- People picture future disasters for others, not themselves (p. 80).
- At least half of purchases can't be explained by function alone (p. 125).
- Desire-building ruled advertising until 1954; identification is the shift since (pp. 121–122).
- You can't contradict an accepted image or belief (pp. 133, 145–146).
- One fully believed promise outsells ten half-believed ones tenfold (p. 158).
- TV ad: about 95% would answer yes to the inclusion question; about 80% of repairs were simple (pp. 149, 151).
- Mail-order products live under two years; an ad runs, then rests three to six months (pp. 115–116).
- One format change can add 50% to readership and results. The Journal-styled ad ran 19 monthly insertions with no drop and had "about twice" the pull (pp. 199, 202).
- A medium's believability matters more than its circulation; layout matters far less than copy (pp. 198–199).
- Never attack a weakness without the fix alongside (p. 189).
- Preface: over $20 million of one Rodale health book sold; Boardroom grew from $3,500 in capital to about $25 million a year; one reader went from $100,000 to $10 million in a year (pp. 4, 7).

## 8. Applications for this brand

All [Inferred]. Brand facts cited are [Stated] in `Brand/` docs.

1. **Add the five-stage sophistication model.** The docs treat sophistication as high or low. The Brand Profile calls this market HIGH (buyers have tried meditation, coaches, Dispenza, the Law of Attraction). In Schwartz's terms that's stage 3 to 5, so bare "calmer life" promises won't land. Two moves fit: lead with *how* (clearing before visualizing; why order matters), or go to identification. Book 2's subtitle ("when you understand everything and still nothing changes") is already a stage-5 headline. A GetHookd pass would show which stage Hungarian competitors write at.

2. **Score desires before picking an angle.** Add urgency, staying power and scope columns to the Angle Map. The method's stated end goal (*nyugalom, béke, megelégedettség*) scores high on staying power and scope for 50+. Money angles score high on urgency but run into the brand's own "financial-crisis trap."

3. **Eight unaware patterns as native-ad lead types.** They extend the seven lead archetypes. Best fits: hidden dream (1), shared resentment (5: "I did everything the books said"), ultimate triumph (6), result of a problem in third person (7), result of an accomplishment (8). All work in first or third person, which is the shape the no-second-person rule needs. The three elimination rules make a quick check for cold native ads.

4. **Gradualization as a lint for VSLs and native ads.** Write out the chain: what does the reader accept at line 1, and what does each block ask next? Find where a new idea arrives unprepared. Here that's usually the hand-position practice or the word "kód." Bring it in the way the TV ad brings in "do it yourself": late, in the same paragraph shape as points already accepted. Swap inclusion questions for first-person statements that earn the same yes.

5. **Simplification for "this sounds hard or strange."** The Sherwin Cody move maps onto brand facts: about 7 minutes, twice a day, a few hand positions, six levels, no therapist. Use only counted numbers already in brand docs.

6. **Escalation for "a book on the subconscious isn't urgent."** Widen the role: the practice sits under every reaction, every day. The "cheap part robbing the expensive car" shape fits: years of effort on the right goals, held back by one thing never cleared first. Land the payoff on "nyugodtabb."

7. **Price mechanism the Collier way.** For Black Friday and Book 2 bundles, give the true reason for the offer (launch, print run, event date) before the number. Anchor only on prices really charged.

8. **Mechanism depth by audience.** Warm audiences: name the Healing Code and move on. Cold: describe it, and make the mechanism copy sell. Cold and sophisticated: put the mechanism in the hook (why visualization on an uncleared subconscious gets "thrown out"). Attribute it as the method's teaching.

9. **Intensification checklist for script middles.** First evening with the book (3); a try-it-tonight test (4); week one to month three (5; the brand says a week to a year); family noticing (6); how little it asks (10); the founder "at your elbow" (11); catalog close (12). Use 7 only with a real expert on record, 13 only with the real guarantee.

10. **Campaign models map onto Andromeda.** Colgate (new opening, same body) is the "visible change in the first 3 seconds" rule. Toni/Clairol (same line, new picture) is a static-iteration recipe. Volkswagen (one theme, a new fact per ad) suits a Book 2 series: one idea, a different character each time.

11. **Identification for 50+ and 65+.** Character roles to test: calm, wise, the steady one. Achievement roles: grandparent, the one who holds the family together. Apply the believability-bridge test to visuals: people and rooms this buyer sees as their own or one step up. The soap failure (p. 138) warns against glossy casting.

12. **Prevention through loved ones.** Frame stakes around family in third person, not "what will happen to you." Family stories stay inside the consent rule.

13. **Deadly sincerity and understatement as default tones.** Burned, skeptical 65+ readers match the market his fourth sample ad was built for. Say who it isn't for and what it doesn't do (not a fix for tomorrow's money problem; a week to a year). Both are brand positions that double as belief builders.

14. **Tag the 38 ways by compliance.** Safe as is: 4–8, 13, 16, 17, 20, 21, 23–25, 29–33, 35, 36. Safe with real sourcing: 1–3, 10, 14, 19, 26. Recast in first or third person: 12, 18, 34, 37, 38. Health-shaped in the book, non-health use only: 9, 11, 15, 22, 27, 28. Fold the safe set into Hooks and the Problem-Solution Headline Writer.

15. **Proof placement audit.** Move testimonials and numbers to where the viewer asks "who says so?": right after the first big promise and again after the mechanism.
