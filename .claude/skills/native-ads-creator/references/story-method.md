# Story Method for Native Ads

The storytelling craft the localizer runs on. Built from the five Copy Foundations docs in `Knowledge/Domain Knowledge/` and the anatomy of W-001 in `Creative/Winners - Ad Library.md`. `SKILL.md` says when each part is used; this file says how.

A native story ad is not a translated ad with the product swapped. It's a story a 50–75-year-old Hungarian reader believes, in which one idea becomes true for her by the end: the one belief. Every rule below serves that.

"She" in this file means the reader, and the narrator in the examples. The narrator can be anyone the source ad made work: a man, a pilot, a daughter. `SKILL.md` Phase 0 step 5 decides who tells the story.

## 1. The twelve beats

This is the spine. Each beat answers one of the reader's silent questions (`One Belief and the Ten Questions.md`). A source ad rarely has all twelve. After the mechanism swap, the rebuilt story must.

The beats sit in **four acts**. "A scene per act" means one in each of these:

| Act | Beats | What the act does |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The opening** | 1–3 | The reader picks herself out and gets a first reason to believe |
| **2. The struggle** | 4–7 | The narrator, what was tried, the real problem, what's to blame |
| **3. The turn** | 8–9 | How it was found, how it works |
| **4. The proof and the close** | 10–12 | What changed, the honest part, the choice handed back |

The acts are jobs, not positions. When a source's order interleaves them (the turn arriving before the real problem, say), "a scene per act" means at least one scene serving each of the four jobs, and "ends leaning forward" is judged at the end of each block of the story as it actually runs.

| # | Beat | Reader's question | What it must contain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Identification opening** | "Is this about me?" | No product, no price, no promise. One of the eight patterns (§2). The reader picks herself out |
| 2 | **What's at stake** | Q2: what do I get? | The wished-for state, shown as a picture, not claimed. One or two lines |
| 3 | **Early proof beat** | Q3: is it real? | One small, concrete thing that changed, with a "but" in it. Before the reader has to take anything on trust |
| 4 | **The narrator arrives** | Q7: why trust you? | Age, a place, a family, a morning. Been in the reader's shoes. One full scene (§3) |
| 5 | **Everything she tried** | Stage 4–5 reader: "I've heard it all" | The rounds she made, named plainly, without bitterness. What she was told |
| 6 | **The real problem** | Q4: what held me back? | The flip: what every earlier attempt was missing. Blame lifted from her, in her own words |
| 7 | **What's to blame** | Q5 | A *thing*: the way we live now, the stress that never gets cleared, the advice to push harder. Never a doctor, never the reader |
| 8 | **The turn** | Q1: what's different? | How she came across it. Small, specific, believable: who mentioned it, where she was, what she thought first (doubt) |
| 9 | **How it works** | Q8 | The problem mechanism first, in kitchen-table words. Then the practice, exact and simplified, next to a familiar picture (§5) |
| 10 | **Proof over time** | Q3 again | Night one: nothing. Then small changes, stretched across weeks, each one a scene. A doubting witness who notices without being told (§4) |
| 11 | **The honest part** | Distrust | What it isn't, how long it takes, that it isn't the same for everyone. One or two lines of plain truth |
| 12 | **The close** | Q6, Q9, Q10 | Where the real material is, the true reason to look now (or none), the choice handed back, the link, and a last line worth keeping (§9) |

Order can flex where the source's order is better (W-001 runs 1, 6, 7, 9, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 12). Two things can't move: **beat 1 opens**, and **the practice in beat 9 is never explained before beats 6 and 7 have landed.**

**A glimpse isn't an explanation.** A character can be *seen* doing something odd early in the story (sitting on the edge of a hotel bed with his fingers held in front of his face; carrying the same dog-eared book to breakfast every day) with no word on what it is. That's curiosity pulling the reader forward, and in a swiped ad it's often the source's best asset. What it is and why it works waits for beat 9.

## 2. The opening: eight patterns

From `Market Awareness and Sophistication.md`. A cold 50+ reader is unaware to problem-aware, in a stage-4-to-5 market. So the opening identifies; it doesn't promise.

**Three eliminations:** no price, no product name, no statement of what the product does.

| Pattern | The opening does this | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden dream | Says the wish she's never said aloud | First person |
| Hidden fear | Names the worry she carries quietly | Narrator's own confession |
| Accepted image | Starts from a picture nobody argues with, to reach a topic she'd resist head-on | Third person / a generation |
| Shared resentment | Voices the grievance she already has | First person |
| Final triumph | Shows the moment it was over, not the road there | A scene |
| Result of the problem | What it cost someone else | Third person |
| Result of the accomplishment | The reward with the effort hidden | A scene |
| Wordless desire | Mood and image; almost no claim | The photo carries it |

**The second-person rule.** "You" is fine when the sentence is about something else (her grandmother, her generation, a question to ask). It's never a statement or question about the reader's health, mind, money or body. W-001's opening talks to "you" about *the grandmother*, and that's why it passes.

**A curiosity loop is not a pattern.** "Something I can't stop thinking about," "what he took out of his bag": that's a device that rides on top of an opening. Keep the source's loop when it's good, and name the pattern underneath it. The loop makes her read on; the pattern is why she thinks it's about her.

**Her own world, early.** When the narrator's life is far from the reader's (a pilot, a surgeon, an estate dealer), the reader needs something of her own in the first third: a spouse at the washing line, a kitchen, a garden barrel. One of the two alternative openings should get there faster than the main one.

Brand material for each pattern: `Strategy/Copy Foundations - Brand Application.md` §6.

## 3. Scene craft: summary versus scene

This is where weak native ads fail. A summary tells the reader something happened. A scene lets her watch it happen. **Every act gets at least one scene.** A scene has:

- **A place and a time of day.** The kitchen at five in the morning. The bench outside the clinic.
- **An object.** The sock, the pill organiser, the garden gate, the phone face-down on the table.
- **A small action.** Not "I was tired" but what tired made her do.
- **One line of speech,** exactly as it was said, where there's another person.

| Summary (weak) | Scene (strong) |
|---|---|
| I was exhausted all the time. | By four in the afternoon I'd be sitting on the edge of the bed with one sock on, working up to the other. |
| The doctors couldn't find anything. | He turned the screen toward me so I could see for myself. Every line in the normal column. "At your age," he said, and reached for the next file. |
| I had tried everything. | There's a drawer in the hall cabinet I don't open when guests are here. Magnesium, a lavender pillow, a relaxation CD still in its wrapper. |
| My husband noticed the difference. | He didn't look up from the crossword. "You're humming," he said. |
| I was sceptical at first. | I read the page twice and thought, well, that's seven minutes I'll never get back. |
| I started to feel calmer. | The kettle boiled and clicked off, and I realised I'd just been standing there, looking at the garden, not making a list. |

Rules:
- **The detail carries the feeling.** Don't name the emotion after showing it.
- **Specific beats general.** A number, a name, a day of the week, a brand of biscuit. Two kinds of number, handled differently: **claims and statistics** (study results, percentages, device readings, prices) need a source in the brain or they're cut; **story-world details** (her age, the hour, how many years in the job) are the narrator's own and only need to be plausible and consistent.
- **Small beats big.** One sock is stronger than "my whole life fell apart."
- **Keep the narrator consistent.** Age, town, family and voice don't drift between acts.
- **Hungarian reality.** The localization rules in `SKILL.md` Phase 2 decide the places, names and objects.

## 4. Proof craft: the "but"

Every proof is three beats: **and** (the setup), **but** (the snag or the doubt), **therefore** (what happened). A proof with no "but" is a claim, and a burned reader doesn't believe claims.

- **Night one is always nothing.** The narrator expects nothing, gets nothing, and says so. That's the "but" that buys belief in everything after.
- **Stretch it over time.** A few mornings later. The end of the first week. The third week. At least two of the steps are full scenes; the others can be a single line each.
- **The change is noticed sideways.** She realises she *didn't* do something she always did. She doesn't announce feeling better.
- **A witness who doubted.** Someone who watched with open scepticism and then remarks on it unprompted, without looking up. The witness says what the narrator can't say about herself.
- **Land on experience, not diagnosis.** What the morning was like, what she did, what someone saw. Under the hard floor: no cure, nothing guaranteed, nothing "gone for good."
- **Put one small proof early** (beat 3) and the long proof late (beat 10).

## 5. Landing the mechanism

The hand-position practice is the strangest idea in the story. It's the point where a reader either keeps going or scrolls. From `Belief Building in Long Copy.md`:

1. **Problem mechanism first.** Stress gets stored; the inner alarm never switches off; the body stays braced. In kitchen-table words. This has to be accepted before the practice is explained. (An unexplained glimpse earlier is fine; see §1.)
2. **The flip.** Everything she tried worked on the surface. Nothing cleared what was stored.
3. **Then the practice, exact:** fingers held 1–2 centimetres from the skin, not touching, over specific points, in a set order, while silently repeating a specific sentence.
4. **Next to something familiar.** A homely comparison the reader already trusts (jump-starting a car battery, *bikázás*).
5. **Simplified with counted facts.** About seven minutes. Morning and evening. Done alone, at home.
6. **In the same plain shape as what came before.** No change of voice, no "amazing," no capital letters. It arrives as one more ordinary thing she did.
7. **Attributed.** It's what the book describes, what the method teaches.

If the practice is *explained* before step 1 has landed, the belief chain is broken. Move the explanation.

## 6. Intensifying the promise

One promise, shown in fresh settings. Use at least four across the story:

| Setting | In a native story |
|---|---|
| In action | The narrator doing the practice, step by step |
| The reader's first day | What the first evening was actually like |
| A test she can run | "Notice tomorrow morning whether…" |
| Stretched over time | Night one, week one, week three, month four |
| An audience | The husband, the daughter on the phone, the neighbour |
| Old way against new | What mornings were, what they are |
| How easy it is | Seven minutes, no equipment, no appointment |
| Metaphor | The battery, the barrel that finally drains, the alarm that goes quiet |

Stop when a setting says nothing the last one didn't.

## 7. Tone: no ad voice

- **Understatement.** Short, plain, few adjectives. A little dry humour at her own expense.
- **Deadly sincerity.** She says what it didn't do, how long it took, that she nearly gave up on it.
- **No selling words.** Nothing "amazing," "incredible," "secret," "breakthrough." No exclamation marks outside speech.
- **Feeling words stay out of sight.** If the reader notices the emotional vocabulary, it's failed. Let objects and actions do it.
- **Blame goes to methods and to the way we live.** Never to doctors or medicine ("the results came back fine" is enough; the doctor did his job). Never to the reader.

## 8. Momentum

- **End each act on an open question** or a half-step forward, so the next act is wanted.
- **Forward phrases** at the joins: the Hungarian equivalents of "and that's when," "what I didn't know yet," "then one Tuesday."
- **Change the kind of copy** when the reader's had enough of one: story, then explanation, then story. Three explanation paragraphs in a row and she's gone.
- **One-line paragraphs** pull the eye down. The formatting rule (one sentence per line) already helps; don't waste it on filler lines.

## 9. The close

- **Where the real thing is.** The official material: the book, the Practical Guide, the Minikurzus, from Lajos Antal and his team. The warning about incomplete versions online is on-brand and true to the practice (the order, the distance and the relaxed state matter).
- **Why now, only if real.** A real offer running now, a real date. Otherwise no urgency line at all.
- **No refund or guarantee talk.**
- **Three roads, in a breath.** Carry on as things are; keep piecing it together alone; or look at the official material. Then give the choice back. No shaming.
- **A generational or identifying question** (W-001: did your grandparents have this at your age?) works as the last turn before the link.
- **The link,** with the emoji-guided line.
- **A line worth keeping.** One sentence with rhythm that sums the story up and can stand alone as a P.S. W-001's: normal and common aren't the same thing.

## 10. Mapping a source ad

Before any swap, take the source apart:

1. **List its beats,** one line each, in order.
2. **Tag each beat** with the twelve-beat number it matches and the reader question it answers.
3. **Name why it worked:** which opening pattern, where the turn is, what the proof rests on, what line people would repeat.
4. **Mark what the swap breaks.** The source's mechanism explanation and its proof are built for its product. Both have to be rebuilt, not reworded.
5. **List the missing beats.** Usually the early proof (3), the honest part (11) and a prepared mechanism (9).
6. **Keep the spine.** The opening pattern, the turn, the rhythm of the proof and the line worth keeping are the source's value. Hold them.

Zero truncation means no source beat's *job* is dropped. It doesn't mean every source paragraph survives at full length:

- Two source beats doing the same job (a recap that repeats the timeline, a second block of product specs) merge into one.
- Blocks can be reordered to hold momentum. The usual fix: bring the honest part up to sit right after the long proof, and keep the run from the witness to the close short. A swiped ad's back half is often where it sags.
- A beat that breaks the wall is replaced by its compliant version, not deleted. `SKILL.md` Phase 1 has the table (guarantee → the honest part; false deadline → the authenticity warning or nothing; cure line → a change in daily experience; and so on).
- Missing beats are added.

Record each decision in the audit's source map: kept, merged, moved, rebuilt or replaced, and why.

## 11. The benchmark: W-001

`Creative/Winners - Ad Library.md` holds the full copy and an eleven-point anatomy. Read it on every run. What it does well: an accepted-image opening about a generation, blame removal early, the problem mechanism before the practice, a familiar analogy, night-one-nothing, proof stretched over five weeks, a doubting husband who notices without looking up, an authenticity warning, a P.S. worth keeping.

What the next ones should do better: a small proof beat in the first third; more scenes and fewer statistics; every number sourced in the brain or cut; the villain kept on the way we live, clear of the doctor's office.
